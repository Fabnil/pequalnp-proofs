# Excluding five uncovered edges at size 58

For the deleted projective plane in [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737), a minimum normalized monotone circuit of size at most 58 must leave **at least six edges** outside its admissible raw AND stars. The [earlier bounds](first-star-five-spare.md) reduce the five-edge case to thirteen centers, all twelve lines and one point $z$, with just one spare union. Here we exclude the remaining branches.

## The last normal form

Every retained edge is singly oriented and the star subgraph spans. A single nonadmissible AND $g$ fires first on all missed edges. Its operands $a,b$ have raw sets on one side with intersection $S$ in the assigned leaves of a good center $c$. Every missing edge meets $S$ and has a different opposite endpoint. Both erased positions are earlier than all destinations after $g$. All other nonadmissible gates have two raw-unreachable operands.

Write $C_v,L_v$ for a good gate's center and leaf positions, using indices if it is split. Write $J$ for the lines assigned from $z$. They carry the point leaf of the relevant good gate at $z$, and their line leaves are absent from $B_z$, the actual raw line leaves at $z$. Raw line supports have their own centers and possibly a point leaf, and raw point supports have line leaves and possibly a center at $z$. The spare union is either one external union $U$, unused in raw ancestries, with no split, or one repeated center slot and no external union. Any ordinary destination is contained in one raw support or is a singleton.

We use [raw-support dominance](first-star-five-saturation.md#setup-and-raw-support-dominance), which forbids a singleton from reaching the full raw support of another vertex. We also use [first difference](first-star-spanning.md#support-and-comparison-bounds-at-an-isolated-vertex). On a positive test that adds positions to a rejecting singleton, the first new gate must use one of those added positions with its mate already available, or use two of the added positions. If a target's only added position is its center, its own gate must run, with its leaf already reached on the singleton.

Before the one spare offer is used, a noncenter singleton following $g$ has one control sequence. A pending center determines the next gate, with its leaf able to stop or permit the step but unable to change the next gate. A raw-unreachable singleton stops without its mate. The same reasoning holds in a pair test as long as its extra target center remains disabled. The spare offer can introduce at most two tracked positions. A repeated offer adds no new tracked positions, since a previously emitted position either is still present or has already been consumed and can no longer be emitted. After the offer, if both tracks follow ordinary single-track successors, each has at most one fixed leaf-only endpoint. If they merge at a fully raw-unreachable gate, both tracked paths have ended at its two operands, and they produce at most one later path instead. We use this to count leaf-only emissions that can occur before a raw target center is enabled.

## Line triggers

The split case with line triggers was already excluded. With an external union there is no split, and $S$ consists of lines in $J$. The raw support of any trigger line $\ell$ contains $L_z,a,b$ and its own center. It cannot reach the center of another line $j$, because the raw support of $j$ consists of that center and a subset of $L_z,a,b$. Every missing point at $\ell$ is different from $z$ and adds only line-leaf positions, without $L_\ell$ because the edge is missing. No first additional gate can use those leaves. In fact each can only use a line center, and the singleton cannot have a center other than its own, whose leaf is absent from the added positions. The missing edge therefore cannot be accepted.

## Point triggers and ordinary targets

Now $S$ consists of points in the leaves of a line $c$. Every missing line is different from $c$, meets $S$ only at its missing point, and has three retained neighbors outside $S$. A missing line is plain if it is outside $J$ and unsplit, so its raw support is just its center. Every plain target leaf must be reached on its trigger singleton, and that good gate is later than $g$.

A plain line retains at least two noncenter points. Their tests start with its good gate, and after that gate they add disjoint good leaf sets to its destination. The erased positions of $g$ are inert. If the destination rejects, it must leave at least two waiting centers to distinguish the two extras. If the line does not contain $z$, it has three such quiet noncenters, and would need three waiting centers. That exceeds the one-spare bound of two. Whenever the destination accepts alone, call the target universal. Then any other singleton of $S$ that reaches its leaf gives a false positive when paired with the line. A single raw point support can supply at most one universal target leaf over the relevant singleton runs. It cannot be used at two different target points, and at a fixed point the supporting raw point shares at most one missing line with it.

For lines in $J$ with the same actual point leaf of $z$, two missing tests require the use of their own line targets. Neither missing point is $z$, and each shares at most one line with $z$, so substitution of the other line would preserve all raw positions except the unread target center slots and would accept a nonedge. A raw center-bearing destination cannot give a leaf of any of these lines. A line support cannot give line leaves, and $C_z$ can offer nothing outside $B_z$.

With $z$ unsplit, this bounds the total misses in $J$ by two. Without a spare offer there is just one common leaf-only destination. With a split pair the two tracks can give at most two destinations, and the pair itself has no $J$ leaves. With an external union offering two tracks, neither operand can give a $J$ leaf, since each must supply one track from a raw support or singleton. If it offers one track, its other operand can supply at most one $J$ leaf and the remaining track can give one further leaf-only destination. If it offers no track, its two operands can give at most two leaves, with no continuing control before the target. Every raw point support gives at most one $J$ leaf by the common-neighbor bound with $z$. The tests that obtain those leaves follow the same control prefix up to the first leaf-only destination or the exceptional offer, as their raw targets are disabled throughout that prefix.

## An external union

There is no split. If $z\in S$, all missed lines are outside $J$. The point $z$ retains an edge from $c$ and another assigned to its own star, hence loses at most two edges. There are at least three misses at noncenters, on lines not incident to $z$ because their missing point and $z$ already share $c$. These targets are universal and no center-bearing ordinary destination can give their leaves. The spare-offer count just used now applies to these leaves as well. If $U$ offers two tracks it contains none of them. If it offers one, it contains at most one target from the untracked operand. If it offers none, two ordinary operands give at most two universal targets. In each case at most two target leaves can be supplied across the runs.

Suppose $z\notin S$, so all trigger points are noncenters. There are at least three plain missing lines, since at most two misses are in $J$. No point singleton can reach a clean line center, meaning one outside $J$, or reach both $L_z$ and a line center in $J$. Either would reproduce a line's raw support.

If a plain good gate has a rejecting destination, its baseline must end with two waiting centers for the two quiet noncenters. This uses the one extra union to supply a pair of good centers. No fully raw-unreachable gate can fire in that baseline, since consuming two tracked positions loses one from the bound of two. No raw-unreachable position can be left at the end, since both positions are needed as good centers. Moreover a normal initial destination cannot continue. It either has no center, or has a single center and unrelated leaves. Thus the good gate with a rejecting destination must emit $U$ itself.

Suppose $U$ cannot be emitted on any point singleton. After $g$ on a trigger singleton a normal line-center offer cannot carry $L_z$, by raw-support dominance. A single $L_z$ without a tracked position stops the run. So a point-center offer cannot be activated. Every needed plain leaf comes from just one common raw point support, either a final leaf-only destination or the leaves accompanying a stalled $C_z$. Partition the plain targets into the ones whose destinations accept alone and the ones with destination $U$. This common support can serve at most one target per class. If it contained two leaves from either class, a point singleton receiving it and accepting one line could accept the other. For the second class the continuation from the point singleton and the destination $U$ is the same. The supporting raw point is already adjacent to both lines, so by the common-neighbor bound it is the only point adjacent to both and neither leaf is missing there. At most two plain targets can be served.

Suppose $U$ is emitted on some point singleton. In this case every plain target is universal. Otherwise the pair of good centers required in $U$ above must consist of centers in $J$, or one of those centers and $C_z$. It cannot contain $L_z$. In the first case the baseline after $U$ has two waiting line centers but no raw point leaves to activate them. A rejecting plain target would need to contain $z$, since otherwise it has three retained noncenter neighbors. None of its quiet noncenters can supply a leaf for a line in $J$, since they are on another line through $z$. In the second case $C_z$ cannot run without $L_z$, and the line center cannot run because the only additional line leaves from its companion operand are in $B_z$, which has no leaves in $J$. The waiting $C_z$ cannot be enabled by a noncenter point. Neither pair can distinguish two quiet neighbors.

It remains to count plain target emissions on the point singletons when $U$ can be used there. Before $U$ they have one control and can emit no line leaf without stopping. A leaf-only emission would stop, and a $C_z$ offer would also stop, because a previous normal emission could not supply $L_z$ while allowing a continuing track. Thus all the relevant singletons follow the same sequence until $U$ or a terminal normal emission. When the latter is the only case there is at most one supporting raw point.

If $U$ offers two tracks and contains a line center, then $L_z$ can never appear on a run that used $U$, by dominance. Both tracks terminate in ordinary leaf destinations, stalled $C_z$ offers, or raw-unreachable slots. An offer of $C_z$ with leaf positions counts as one terminal leaf destination. It also counts as a terminal track if provided by $U$ itself. Each track gives at most one raw point support with target leaves. If the two controls end at raw-unreachable mates, the merged track can give at most one such support instead. If a two-track $U$ contains no line center, it offers $C_z$ and a raw-unreachable operand, or two raw-unreachable operands. In the former case neither operand can carry $L_z$, so both tracks stall and only the leaves accompanying $C_z$ can help. In the latter case only their possible common gate can continue, with one track and no preceding line leaves.

If $U$ offers one track which is a line center, its other operand can provide one raw point leaf set, and the continuing line track can give at most one other before stopping. If $U$ offers only $C_z$ as its track, it either lacks $L_z$ and stops with at most two raw point supports, or its other operand supplies $L_z$ and therefore no line leaves. In the latter case $h_z$ can run, but no line center can be emitted after $L_z$ is reached. It gives at most one further leaf destination beyond $B_z$. A single inaccessible track or no track cannot continue. These cases supply at most two raw point supports with target leaves, which cannot cover three universal plain targets.

## A split star

Now $E=0$ and $R=1$. Every unsplit plain target is universal. The baseline from its destination cannot progress at all unless it already accepts. A center-bearing raw support offers slots of one center and no leaf belonging to any of those slots. A leaf-only support or a raw-unreachable singleton cannot continue either. Thus at most one geometric center can wait in a rejecting baseline. It cannot serve the disjoint extra leaf sets of two retained noncenter points.

Suppose the split star is at a line $j$, so $z$ is unsplit. At most two misses are in $J$ by the earlier bound. If $z\notin S$, this leaves at least three misses outside $J$, of which at most one is a split target. If $z\in S$, none of the missing lines is in $J$. The point $z$ loses at most two edges, having a retained edge from $c$ and a different edge of its own star. This leaves at least three noncenter misses on lines not incident to $z$.

If $j$ is outside $J$, a point singleton cannot reach both slots of $j$, whose raw support is just those slots. So it has at most one usable track despite the split. On a noncenter point singleton $C_z$ cannot be activated, since obtaining $L_z$ would require either a line-centered offer from an unsplit line in $J$, forbidden by dominance, or a leaf-only emission after which there is no continuing track. The one terminal raw point support cannot serve the at least two unsplit plain targets.

If $j$ is in $J$ and $z\in S$, the at least three noncenter targets are unsplit and plain. No center-bearing offer can supply them, since their lines do not contain $z$. One split-pair offer leads to at most two fixed leaf endpoints, which is insufficient.

If $j$ is in $J$ and $z\notin S$, all the at least three plain targets are unsplit. If no relevant singleton reaches both slots in a split-pair offer, the single control can supply leaves only with the one offer of $C_z$ or with its final leaf-only destination, giving at most two universal targets. Otherwise a singleton using the pair cannot ever reach $L_z$, as the pair with $L_z$ is the whole raw support of $j$. In particular the common sequence to the pair has no earlier leaf-only emission supplying line leaves, nor can it pass through an earlier offer of $C_z$ that would need $L_z$ to continue. Thus the successful singleton runs that supply the plain targets must reach this same split-pair offer before any target leaves.

After the pair is offered, at most one of its centers can run initially, since the point has at most one raw leaf slot at $j$ by unique orientation. The other head can obtain its leaf only from a leaf-only emission, as $j\in J$ and $B_z$ has none of its leaves. While both slots have been reached, any subsequent $C_z$ offer is stalled for lack of $L_z$. A path can therefore contribute one leaf-only destination or one stalled offer of $C_z$ with line leaves. The other path can contribute at most one more, and the two paths can instead merge through raw-unreachable mates with at most one later leaf destination. At most two universal plain targets can be supplied.

Finally suppose the split star is at $z$. Its assigned lines have disjoint groups $J_1,J_2$ according to their raw point leaf $L_{z,1}$ or $L_{z,2}$. We claim there is at most one miss in each group. For two misses in the same $J_i$, both tests must use their target line centers by substitution. Each missing point is a noncenter and its raw support has no leaf for any line in $J_1\cup J_2$, since it shares just its missing line with $z$. A center-bearing emission also cannot supply any of those line leaves.

Before the first emission of line leaves without a center, the control sequence is single-track until the split pair is offered, if it is offered at all. The only raw point leaf in the pair test is $L_{z,i}$. An earlier emission offering $L_{z,k}$ for $k\ne i$ would have no control or would offer a line from $J_k$, which cannot run for lack of its leaf. Thus if the split pair is offered, only its $i$ center is initially enabled. The other center can be activated only by a later emission of $L_{z,k}$. Such an emission either ends the first track or offers a disabled line from $J_k$. At most one control continues, and any dormant line it leaves behind is in $J_k$.

The first line-leaf-only destination can contain at most one leaf for a line through $z$. If it supplies a target in $J_i$, it cannot also activate a dormant line of $J_k$, and it cannot supply a point leaf to activate a dormant center of $z$. If it instead activates the dormant line in $J_k$, it supplies no target in $J_i$, and there is just one continuing control left to supply the target. Both tests of type $J_i$ must thus obtain their target from one common raw point support, which can give at most one target in $J_i$. This proves the claim.

For $z\notin S$ there are at least three plain missing targets. For $z\in S$ no missing line belongs to $J$ and $z$ loses at most one edge, since it retains an edge from $c$ and one assigned from each half of its own star. There are then at least four plain targets at noncenters. In either case all the relevant lines are unsplit and universal. On a noncenter point singleton neither $L_{z,i}$ can be produced by a normal center-bearing destination, because the line center supplying it would give the whole raw support of a line in $J_i$. A leaf-only emission of such a guard would end the one track before the first point-center offer. Thus the split pair or any individual point-center offer stalls when reached. There is only one raw point support producing line leaves on the common singleton sequence, either a final leaf-only destination or the leaves accompanying that stalled point-center offer. It cannot supply the universal targets.

This excludes the last five-edge branch. The [six-edge budget](first-star-six-budget.md) gives further restrictions when the retained stars lose a matching of size twelve. The existence of a 58-gate circuit remains open.
