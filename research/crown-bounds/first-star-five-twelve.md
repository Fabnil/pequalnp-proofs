# Excluding twelve centers with five uncovered edges

Suppose a minimum normalized circuit of size at most 58 for [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737) has exactly five edges outside its admissible raw AND stars $H$. Then it has at least thirteen distinct star centers.

## Setup and ordinary missing tests

Suppose there are twelve centers. By the [cover restriction](first-star-five-cover.md), they are all lines. The star subgraph spans and a single nonadmissible gate $g$ with operands $a,b$ fires first on every missing edge. Its singleton triggers form a nonempty point set $S$. There are $w=2$ or $3$ erased positions that are raw-reachable. If there is a third position $d$, it is an operand of another gate $f$ whose other operand is raw-unreachable. All other nonadmissible gates have two raw-unreachable operands.

Write $A$ for the number of unions with an erased operand in raw ancestries, $R$ for repeated center slots, and $E$ for unions outside raw ancestries. We have $w\le A\le4$ and $R+E\le4-A$. A support in a raw ancestry consists of part of one raw point's leaf and erased positions, or part of one raw line's center and erased positions. As before, the tracked positions are actual center positions and raw-unreachable operands.

Call a missing test $(x,\ell)$ ordinary if the line's entire raw support is one center position $C_\ell$. Then $x\in S$, its singleton reaches $L_\ell$, and the good gate $h_\ell$ must fire after $g$ on the positive test. In fact adding $C_\ell$ to the rejecting singleton can change the test only through that gate. If $S$ lies in the assigned leaves of a single line, each ordinary missing line meets $S$ only at its missing point and retains three neighbors outside $S$. Their tests start with $h_\ell$. After $h_\ell$, the common baseline is its destination. The three retained tests add pairwise disjoint sets of good leaves, with $L_\ell$ already consumed. The positions of $g$ are inert, and the tests may also add $d$ if it exists. If the common baseline rejects, the [first-difference argument](first-star-spanning.md#support-and-comparison-bounds-at-an-isolated-vertex) needs three waiting centers for three disjoint good leaf sets, except that a waiting mate of $d$ can distinguish the tests that supply $d$.

Call an ordinary target leaf universal if the corresponding good gate accepts from its destination alone. Whenever $S$ is contained in one star's assigned leaves, emitting such a target leaf on a singleton of $S$ distinct from its missing point gives a false positive. A leaf destination contained in a single raw point support can supply at most one universal target leaf across the singleton runs at the missing points. It cannot serve more than one point. At a fixed point $x$, its supporting raw point cannot share two missing lines with $x$. The supporting point itself has none of its missing leaves in raw support.

## A bound on the leaf destinations of singleton points

We use a short continuation argument in the cases with at most one exceptional offer of several tracked positions. Outside that offer, a destination can give a single tracked position, or leaves with no tracked position. Extra raw $d$ positions, when present, are guards and do not add to the tracked count. With one tracked position, the next gate is fixed by that position. A center needs its leaf, and the raw-unreachable operand of $f$ needs $d$. A different raw-unreachable singleton needs another tracked position and stops if there is none. A leaf-only emission consumes the last track and ends the run. Thus the singleton points that obtain a target leaf follow the same sequence from $g$ up to the first exceptional offer or a common leaf destination.

The exceptional offer can be one external union, or the two slots of a split star in a raw support. If it occurs, the runs in question all reach it before any target leaves have been produced. A split-line offer has no leaves. If an external union offers a universal target leaf and the runs involve at least two different points, it gives a false positive, so none of those leaves can be obtained from that offer. Following the offer there are at most two tracks. Re-emitting the exceptional support adds no new tracks. Each track has a fixed forward sequence of successors, with raw guards able to stop or delay it but unable to change its destination. Each can end in one leaf set. If the two paths instead end at the two raw-unreachable operands of a common gate, they merge and produce at most one further path, with no leaf set previously emitted by either path. Thus at most two fixed ordinary leaf destinations can supply target leaves over all the runs. This also applies when the exceptional offer is a fixed pair of slots with possible changes in the untracked guard $d$, which do not create new tracks.

## Four erased unions

If $A=4$, we have $R=E=0$. First assume there is an erased position in a raw line support. We claim that $S$ is in one star's assigned leaves and at least three missing tests are ordinary.

If a mixed attachment carries both $a,b$, the [one-fork reduction](first-star-five-cover.md#singleton-triggers-with-all-line-centers) requires at least two pure unions. There are at most two mixed attachments. The combined attachment belongs to a point group, since there are no singleton line triggers. Any raw line with an erased position uses the one other attachment, which cannot carry both $a,b$. Every missing edge must use the point group, and the affected line has at most one missing edge.

Otherwise the attachments for $a$ and $b$ are separate. At least one attachment for each goes to the point side. If at least two lines have erased positions, there are at most two point attachments, one for each of $a,b$. The intersection $S$ lies in one point group. A line supplying one operand needs the point endpoint to supply the other, so each of the at most two affected lines has at most one missing edge. A line supplying neither has its missing points in $S$. If exactly one line has an erased position, there are at most three point attachments, so one of $a,b$ has a single point attachment and again $S$ lies in one point group. The affected line can have at most two missing edges, since the point operand it needs is supplied by at most two groups. This proves the claim.

For an ordinary missing line, a rejecting baseline from the good gate's destination has at most one waiting position. To accept all three retained tests, $d$ must exist and be supplied by all three retained points, since their other extra leaf sets are disjoint. At most two mixed attachments can carry $d$. Indeed for $w=3,A=4$ the pure erased graph with $P$ pure nodes has at least $3-P$ components feeding $4-P$ mixed attachments. Each component feeds at least one. If $d$ reaches all three points, one attachment reaches at least two and must have common retained label $L_\ell$. Each attachment can oblige this test at only one line. At most two ordinary target leaves can therefore fail to be universal.

There is at least one universal target, and at least three ordinary targets in total. All of their point singletons must reach the same leaf destination $T$, since there is no exceptional offer. As $T$ is in one raw point support, it can supply at most one target at a given point. So at least two distinct points receive $T$. A universal target then gives a false positive at the other point.

For the rest of the $A=4$ case suppose there are no erased positions at raw lines. Every line is ordinary in the sense of having raw support $\{C_\ell\}$. The set $S$ lies in the union of at most two point groups of assigned leaves. If some attachment carries both $a,b$, there are at most two mixed attachments. Otherwise the less frequent operand occurs in at most two attachments, whose groups cover $S$.

Raw-support dominance forbids reaching any center on a point singleton. Every missing leaf must come from one fixed destination $T$, either directly from $g$ or from $f$ after $g$ emits its unreachable mate. No other gate can supply a leaf on such a singleton, as there is no center and at most one tracked position. Further, any good gate $h_\ell$ must emit the output or a raw-unreachable singleton. To see this, every positive line test first differs from its point singleton by firing $h_\ell$. If its destination belongs to another raw support, substituting that vertex for the line would accept all four neighbors of $\ell$, which the graph forbids. If $T$ comes directly from $g$, there is no unreachable position in the point singleton closure, so the only useful unreachable emission by a good gate of a missing line is the one for $f$. If $T$ comes from $f$, the only unreachable position in the singleton closure is that operand of $f$ itself, whose mate is raw-reachable. It cannot help another unreachable emission. Hence at most two distinct good-gate destinations are used by missing lines, the output and possibly the unreachable operand of $f$.

For each of these destination classes, $T$ can supply only one missing line. If it contains the leaves of two missing lines with the same destination, any point that accepts either line and reaches $T$ accepts both. But the supporting point of $T$ is retained at both lines, and two lines share at most one common point. That point has neither missing leaf. A fixed missing line has at most two missing neighbors in $S$, one per group of assigned leaves. Two classes therefore account for at most four missing edges.

## Three erased unions

Here $R+E\le1$. If $w=2$ and some mixed attachment carries both $a,b$, there can be only one mixed attachment, on the point side. If none does, at least two point attachments supply the singleton triggers and at most one line can have erased positions. With a line attachment, the two point attachments each serve one operand, so the affected line loses at most one edge. In every case $S$ is contained in one star's assigned leaves and at least four missing lines have no erased positions. Removing a possible split line leaves at least three ordinary targets. Their good gates accept from the destination alone, since the baseline has at most two tracked positions and the three retained tests add disjoint raw leaf sets.

If $w=3$, the erasure count is tight. Each erased position enters one point group or one singleton line. Both $a,b$ occur only on the point side because they intersect at a singleton point. Thus all missing edges are at distinct lines and their missing points lie in one point group containing $S$. If $d$ is on the line side, at most one line has erased positions, and all ordinary targets are universal by the three disjoint retained tests. If $d$ is on the point side, the only way a rejecting good gate can serve three retained tests from at most two waiting positions is for at least two points to supply $d$ to the same waiting mate. Its attachment must then have label $L_\ell$. This can happen at at most one line.

In all these cases there are at least three universal ordinary targets. If $E=1$ there are at least four, since there is no split line to remove. Then at least two distinct points are involved because a point loses at most three edges. Thus an exceptional external offer cannot supply a universal target on their common initial sequence. A split-line offer supplies no leaves. The bound of two ordinary leaf destinations is too small for the universal targets.

## Two erased unions

Now $w=2$, $R+E\le2$, and each of $a,b$ has one mixed attachment, on the point side. All five missing edges have points in $S$, which lies in one star's assigned leaves, and they use distinct lines. Every unsplit line has raw support $\{C_\ell\}$. A point singleton cannot reach any unsplit center or all the actual center slots of any split line, by raw-support dominance.

If $E=0$, the baseline from the destination of an ordinary missing line's good gate cannot continue at all unless it already has the output. Its destination is a singleton unreachable position, leaves alone, or only center positions of one line. The operands of $g$ are earlier and cannot appear. A single group of center positions can distinguish at most one of the three retained tests, since their extra leaf labels are disjoint in projection. All ordinary targets are therefore universal. If there are two split lines, each has two slots and a point singleton can reach at most one from each group. Its emissions never offer multiple tracked positions at once. If there is just one split line with three slots, the first offer of two slots is common to all continuing singleton runs. The third slot cannot be reached on a run that receives that pair, and a repeat offer adds no tracked positions. Thus the one-exception bound applies. In every case there are at least three ordinary targets, beyond the at most two available leaf destinations.

Now let $E>0$, so at most one line is split and there are at least four ordinary targets. Suppose some ordinary target is not universal. Its rejecting baseline must end with three distinct waiting centers to serve its retained tests. This takes all the slack in the tracking bound $1+R+E$. In particular no gate consuming two tracked positions can fire in the baseline, since the same tracking proof subtracts one for every such consumption. Every raw-unreachable position that appears in the baseline would then remain pending, but the three final positions must all be good centers. Thus no raw-unreachable operand is reached.

Every external union must be used in that baseline and must contain at least two good center positions. Otherwise the tracking proof can stop its backwards expansion at external supports containing at most one tracked position, charging no union for such supports, or can skip unused unions. The reduced count would give fewer than three waiting centers. Each external union therefore contains an unsplit center or both slots of the one split line, so it cannot be used on any point singleton. Without external unions or split pairs available on those singletons, they can obtain targets only from one common raw point destination $T$. It must contain at least four target leaves of distinct unsplit lines. Four such leaves fill the supporting point's entire good raw set, since its original degree is at most four. The other raw positions there are among $a,b$, already present on the singleton receiving $T$. This reproduces the supporting point's full raw support at a different point, contrary to dominance. If five leaves are needed, even the degree bound alone suffices.

Finally suppose all ordinary targets are universal. If $R=0$, point singleton runs reach no centers at all. The new leaf positions after $g$ come from its fixed destination and any later gates that run solely on unreachable slots. Those emissions are independent of the starting leaves. Thus all points in $S$ receive the same targets. At least two distinct points are needed and this gives a false positive. If $R=1$, then $E=1$ and the split pair cannot be emitted on a point singleton. The only exceptional offer is the one external union. At least four ordinary targets require at least two points, and universality forbids the common external offer to supply any of them. The at most two other leaf destinations cannot suffice.

All cases with twelve centers are excluded. The [thirteen-center analysis](first-star-five-thirteen.md) excludes equality at thirteen centers and gives the remaining parameters for thirteen and fourteen centers. The existence of a circuit at size 58 remains open.
