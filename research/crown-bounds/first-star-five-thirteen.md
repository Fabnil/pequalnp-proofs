# Excluding saturation with thirteen centers and five uncovered edges

Suppose a minimum normalized circuit of size at most 58 for [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737) has exactly five edges outside its admissible raw AND stars. If there are thirteen distinct centers, the projection-and-erasure bound cannot be tight. In that case the size is 58, each retained edge has just one orientation, and exactly two nonadmissible operand positions are raw-reachable. The last restriction also holds in the fourteen-center case by its budget.

## Equality and the possible placements of centers

The [five-edge restrictions](first-star-five-spanning.md) give a spanning star graph $H$ and one nonadmissible AND $g$ firing first on all missing edges. At equality every raw vertex has at most one good center position, and every nonsingleton destination is contained in a raw support. The raw sets reaching $a,b$ at $g$ must intersect. Otherwise each would be in one star's assigned leaves or be a singleton, and the missing edges between them would be a matching of size at most four by the common-neighbor bound. The intersection $S$ is on a single physical side and is contained in the assigned leaves of a center $c$. The five missing edges have their endpoint on that side in $S$ and have distinct endpoints on the opposite side.

The optional third raw-reachable erased position $d$ is an operand of a gate whose other operand is raw-unreachable. At equality $d$ has just one mixed attachment, and its raw set shares a retained label. If $d$ exists, there are no doubly oriented edges of $H$. If it is absent, there can be at most one doubly oriented edge by the budget.

The [noncenter neighbor bound](first-star-five.md#a-constraint-on-missing-neighbors-of-noncenters) forbids an unchosen vertex from having two missing edges to other unchosen vertices. We checked the possible placements under these geometric conditions in [first_star_five_geometry.py](first_star_five_geometry.py), using the explicit edge list in [PlaneCompletion.lean](PlaneCompletion.lean). The checker runs with Python 3. It chooses the center $c$ and five deleted edges at its neighbors, excluding the edges to $c$. It discards any isolated vertex and enumerates all subsets of omitted lines. A retained neighbor of an omitted line must be a point center. For point triggers, a point with at least two missing edges to omitted lines must also be a center. For line triggers, every omitted line requires choosing all but at most one of its missing points. Those additional sets of points are disjoint across lines because the endpoints opposite the trigger side are distinct. The checker forces $c$ into the cover in both cases. It uses only necessary conditions, so orientation choices need not be enumerated.

| 13 | Points | 0 |
| 13 | Lines | 0, 12 |
| 14 | Points | 0, 1, 10, 11 |
| 14 | Lines | 0, 1, 10, 11 |

We exclude the two placements for thirteen centers below.

## All points are centers

There is no doubly oriented edge. The trigger side must be lines, since two raw points have disjoint projected supports and a singleton group could not account for five deletions. Write $z$ for the point whose assigned leaves contain $S$. There are five distinct missing points, each with at least two retained line neighbors. At most one of the five raw points supplies $d$, since a point support has only its center as a retained label. Keep the four or five tests whose point adds only its center $C_v$ to the singleton line. Their good gate $h_v$ must fire after $g$, so the line singleton reaches $L_v$.

There is at most one tracked position after $g$ on a line singleton. A center-bearing destination contains no good leaf. A raw-unreachable position can continue only through its raw mate $d$, if it has one. A destination containing a good leaf ends the run, since it has no tracked position. Thus all the kept tests require the same leaf destination $T$ in their line singleton runs. The set $T$ lies in one raw line support. It can contain at most one missing point's leaf for each trigger line, since two distinct lines share at most one point. So the kept triggers are distinct lines. If there are five, this exceeds the degree of $z$.

If there are just four, $d$ is on the point side and absent from each kept point. The retained tests at a kept point $v$ have lines outside $S$, since the only line in $S$ incident to $v$ is its missing one. They start with $h_v$, and after it fire from a common destination and disjoint extra raw leaf sets. There is no raw $d$ on these tests. Two positive tests with disjoint extras cannot be distinguished from a rejecting common baseline with at most one tracked position. Thus the destination alone accepts. A different kept trigger line also reaches $L_v$ from $T$, so pairing it with $v$ wrongly accepts.

## All lines and one point are centers

Write $z$ for the point center, and $J$ for the lines to which its star assigns an edge. A raw line $\ell$ has $C_\ell$ and has $L_z$ exactly when $\ell\in J$. A raw point $x\ne z$ has only line leaves. The point $z$ has $C_z$ and line leaves $B_z$ for edges assigned from line centers to $z$. At most one line in $J$ can also have its leaf in $B_z$, corresponding to the possible doubly oriented edge. These descriptions omit erased positions.

### Line triggers

Here $S\subseteq J$. Every missing point is different from $z$ and is a noncenter. For a fixed trigger line $\ell$, the missing points add pairwise disjoint good leaf sets, since they share $\ell$ and none has its leaf. If $d$ has a point-side attachment, two such points cannot share it either, since that would require a shared retained label. Relative to the rejecting singleton at $\ell$, each missing point must use a different waiting tracked position. The singleton starts with one center and can have at most two tracked positions after $g$. So each line loses at most two edges and at least three distinct lines are involved.

If another line in $S$ has the same or a larger raw set of erased positions as $\ell$, a true test at $\ell$ must use $h_\ell$. An accepting computation that did not use that gate would survive the change to the other line, since it replaces only the unread center and possibly adds $d$. This would accept a nonedge because a missing point is incident to just one line of $S$. The only line for which this comparison can fail is the unique line in $S$ carrying $d$, if there is one. When there is a doubly oriented edge there is no $d$. In that case remove the possible line in $S$ whose leaf belongs to $B_z$. Either way at least two distinct target centers remain required.

Each kept target lacks its leaf until some gate emits it. The raw point supplies no other leaf of a line in $S$, since it meets just its missing line there. No center-bearing destination can add a kept target leaf. A line center can offer $L_z$ but no line leaves, and the center of $z$ can offer only the line leaves in $B_z$. Besides the disabled target center, there is at most one tracked position after $g$. Hence all these successful tests follow the same sequence of control offers until one common leaf destination enables their target lines. Such a destination lies in one raw point support, which supplies at most one kept target leaf. For $z$ this follows from the exclusion of the double orientation, and for every other point from the common-neighbor bound with $z$. This is impossible.

### Point triggers

Now $S$ is in the assigned leaves of a line $c$. The five missing lines are distinct and each has three retained neighbors outside $S$. Call a missing test plain if its point is different from $z$ and its raw line adds only its center. This means the line is outside $J$ and has no $d$. Every plain test needs its line leaf in the rejecting point singleton, and its good gate follows $g$.

For a plain missing line, at least two of its retained points are noncenters. Their tests start with the good gate at the line. The common baseline after it is its destination, and the extra good leaf sets of these two tests are disjoint after removing the common line leaf. With at most one tracked position left, a rejecting baseline can serve both tests only if both supply the same $d$ to a waiting mate. The attachment of $d$ would then have to contain the line's leaf as its common retained label. Therefore at most one plain target can fail to have an accepting destination, and such a failure requires $d$ on the point side.

Suppose first that $z\\notin S$. Consider missing lines in $J$, except the possible one whose leaf also belongs to $B_z$. If two have the same raw $d$ status, the missing points on them are distinct because both lines contain $z$. Each true test must use its own center. Otherwise replacing its line with the other would preserve acceptance on a nonedge, since both have $L_z$ and the same erased positions. Before the first leaf emission enabling a target, neither a line center nor the center of $z$ can offer a kept target leaf. With at most one control other than the disabled target, the two successful tests must follow the same prefix to a leaf-only destination. That destination can supply at most one kept leaf for a line through $z$, by the same reasoning as in the line-trigger case. Therefore at most one kept missing line lies in $J$ for each raw $d$ status.

If there is a double orientation, this bounds the total losses in $J$ by two, and leaves at least three plain tests. With neither a double orientation nor $d$, at most one missing line is in $J$. If $d$ attaches to a line outside $J$, it can reach only one such line and all lines in $J$ have the same status. This also leaves at least three plain tests. If $d$ attaches only within $J$, there are at most two missing lines in $J$ and the other three are plain. If $d$ attaches to points, at most one missing line is in $J$, leaving at least four plain tests. In all cases at least three plain target leaves have accepting good-gate destinations.

On a point singleton of a plain test there is at most one tracked position. Its control sequence can emit line leaves either with one offer of $C_z$ or in a leaf-only destination at the end. The former offer can occur only once in the sequence, since the only continuation from it consumes $C_z$ and later emissions cannot include it again. Other center offers may carry $L_z$ but no line leaves. Missing guards can stop the fixed sequence but cannot change it into another. Thus there are at most two fixed destinations supplying line leaves over all the relevant singleton runs. For the target lines with accepting destinations, any one such emission serves at most one missing point, because serving another point of $S$ with the same leaf would accept a nonedge. It can serve at most one missing line at a fixed point, because its supporting raw point has at most one common line with that point, or is the same point and has no missing leaf. Two destinations cannot serve three such targets.

Now suppose $z\\in S$. No missing line lies in $J$. A missed edge at $z$ cannot be assigned to its star, and any other missing point in $S$ shares $c$ with $z$ and has no other common line. When $d$ exists there is no double orientation. Then $z$ retains its edge from $c$ and an edge assigned to its own star, so it loses at most two edges. With no $d$ it loses at most three. Consequently there are at least two plain noncenter tests even after discarding a possible line carrying $d$. If $d$ is on the point side there are at least three such tests, and at most one has a good gate that fails to accept from its destination. Thus at least two plain targets have accepting good-gate destinations. The offer of $C_z$ contributes none of these leaves, since the target lines at points other than $z$ do not contain $z$. There is only one leaf-only destination over the successful singleton runs. It cannot serve two such targets, by the same common-neighbor and false-positive argument.

This excludes equality with thirteen centers. The [budget](first-star-five.md#consequences-of-saturation) then forces size 58, $w=2$, and $\sigma=43$ in the thirteen-center branch. The fourteen-center branch still has equality, with $w=2$ and $\sigma=43$. The existence of a circuit at size 58 remains open.
