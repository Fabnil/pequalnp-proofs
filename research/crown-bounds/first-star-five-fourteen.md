# Excluding fourteen centers with five uncovered edges

Suppose a minimum normalized circuit of size at most 58 for [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737) has exactly five edges outside its admissible raw AND stars. Then there are exactly thirteen distinct centers. The [previous analysis](first-star-five-thirteen.md) leaves only thirteen or fourteen centers, with size 58 and two raw-reachable erased operands. We exclude fourteen here.

## Equality and the comparisons used below

With fourteen centers the count is tight. Every center has one good gate, every edge of $H$ has one orientation, and every nonsingleton destination belongs to a raw support. There is a unique nonadmissible gate $g$ that fires first on the missing edges. Both its raw operands are on the same physical side, and their intersection $S$ is a subset of the assigned leaves of one good center $c$. Every missing edge meets $S$, and its opposite endpoint has just that one neighbor in $S$. In particular the five opposite endpoints are distinct. These are the [equality conditions](first-star-five-thirteen.md#equality-and-the-possible-placements-of-centers).

Use $C_v,L_v$ for the center and leaf positions of the good gate $h_v$. Let $B_v$ be the good leaf positions in raw $v$. After $g$, the two erased positions are no longer needed. A center-bearing destination consists of its offered center and possibly leaves from the same raw vertex. The remaining nonadmissible gates have two raw-unreachable operands.

After $g$ on a singleton noncenter there is at most one tracked position, meaning a good center or a raw-unreachable operand. Every following firing consumes a tracked position and emits at most one. As long as just one track is available, the next gate is fixed. Its guard can make the run stop but cannot select another gate. In particular, on missing tests with a raw target center and a noncenter trigger, this same single control sequence applies until the target receives its missing leaf. It also applies until an additional leaf position in the raw target support is read. A leaf-only destination ends a single-track run. During a sequence of control offers each center can be offered at most once, since the only continuation consumes that center and later rules cannot emit it again.

We repeatedly use two comparisons. First, a singleton cannot reach the whole raw support of another vertex, by [raw-support dominance](first-star-five-saturation.md#setup-and-raw-support-dominance). Thus a noncenter in $S$ cannot have just one retained neighbor. Its entire raw support would be the common leaf $L_c$ and the operands of $g$, already supplied by every other point or line in $S$. There are at least two members of $S$ since five missed edges cannot all be incident to one vertex. Also, a trigger with at least three missing neighbors that are noncenters would need three waiting centers in its rejecting singleton run. The missing neighbors' added raw leaf sets are disjoint because they already share the trigger vertex and do not retain its edge. There are at most two waiting centers even if the trigger itself is a center.

Second, suppose $v$ is a missing target center opposite $S$, and $h_v$ is later than $g$. If $v$ has two retained noncenter neighbors, they are outside $S$ and their tests start with $h_v$. After it fires, their common baseline consists of its destination and $B_v$. Their extra good leaves are disjoint after consuming $L_v$, since they already share $v$. That baseline can have at most one tracked position, so it cannot reject and require the disjoint extras to accept both tests. Thus reaching $h_v$ with $B_v$ available suffices to accept. Call $v$ plain if its raw support is just $C_v$. A missing test at a plain target must reach $L_v$ on its trigger singleton, and hence has $h_v$ later than $g$. If such a plain target has two retained noncenters, emitting its leaf on another singleton of $S$ gives a false positive.

In particular, when the triggers for a collection of such plain targets are noncenters, all its missing tests must have the same trigger vertex. The first destination that emits any of their target leaves occurs along the common single-control sequence of their singleton runs. Every singleton needing one of these leaves must reach that first emission. The first emitted leaf permits only its one missing neighbor in $S$. Also, any one destination in a raw support can supply at most one missing target leaf at a fixed trigger. Its supporting vertex and the trigger would otherwise share two missing neighbors. If it is the trigger's own support, it supplies no missing leaf.

The [geometry check](first-star-five-thirteen.md#equality-and-the-possible-placements-of-centers) gives $0,1,10,11$ omitted lines.

## One or ten omitted lines

These two cases admit the same comparison after choosing the sides appropriately. Let side $A$ have all its vertices chosen except $u$, and side $B$ have $m$ chosen centers. If one line is omitted, $u$ is that line and $m=3$ chosen points. If ten lines are omitted, $u$ is the one unchosen point and $m=2$ chosen lines. We first show that all $m$ centers of $B$ are adjacent to $u$ in $G$, and that $u$ has at least two retained edges.

All retained edges of $u$ go to chosen vertices of $B$. If the trigger side is $A$, then $u$ belongs to $S$, since its degree is greater than $m$ and hence it has a missing edge. It retains at least two edges by raw-support dominance. For $m=2$ these are both chosen neighbors. If $u$ had degree four it would have two missing noncenter neighbors, violating the [noncenter bound](first-star-five.md#a-constraint-on-missing-neighbors-of-noncenters). So only a degree-three $u$ is possible. For $m=3$, the omitted line retains two or three edges. If it retains two, one of its two missing neighbors must be the third chosen point by the same bound. Either way all three chosen points are on the omitted line.

If the trigger side is $B$, then $u$ is opposite and loses at most one edge. Since its degree exceeds $m$, it loses exactly one, retains all $m$ chosen neighbors, and has degree $m+1$. In each case the claimed geometry follows.

### Triggers on side A

The common center $c$ is among the chosen vertices of $B$ and is retained at $u$. All other vertices in $S$ are centers with just $L_c$ as their raw good leaf, because any other chosen vertex in $B$ already shares $u$ with $c$. Their missing neighbors are noncenters and are not adjacent to $u$. At least three missed edges use triggers other than $u$, as $u$ retains at least two. Each trigger can account for at most two of these tests, so there are at least two distinct trigger centers among them.

Each positive test at one of those trigger centers $x$ must use $h_x$. Otherwise substituting raw $u$ for $x$ would only remove the unread $C_x$ and possibly add leaves, and would accept a nonedge. No center-bearing destination can offer the leaf of such a target $x$. The only chosen opposite vertex adjacent to $x$ is $c$, and the $c$ star assigns the edge to $x$, so $L_x$ is not in $B_c$. Before the target is enabled there is one control besides that target after $g$, since its added opposite vertex is a noncenter. The successful tests must all reach the same leaf-only destination that supplies the target leaves. It lies in one raw support on side $B$, which can supply at most one such leaf by the common-neighbor bound with $c$.

### Triggers on side B

Let $x_*$ be the unique missing neighbor of $u$. Then $x_*\in S$, and $c$ and $u$ share $x_*$. All $m$ centers on side $B$ are retained neighbors of $u$, so none can be adjacent to $c$. Thus all triggers in $S$ are noncenters. The raw support of $u$ consists of the $m$ leaf positions of those chosen centers. The accepting test $(x_*,u)$ requires a waiting center $C_z$ of one of them in the rejecting singleton at $x_*$. This singleton stalls there without $L_z$. Up to this stall, any other trigger singleton follows the same control sequence or stops earlier. It cannot pass the stall, since a raw vertex on side $B$ cannot supply a leaf position of another vertex on that same side, and the emissions along the common sequence are fixed.

Any other missing target is a center $v$ on side $A$ and is adjacent to at most one of the chosen vertices of $B$, since any two of those already share $u$. Its raw good leaves are either empty, for a plain target, or just $L_{z'}$ for that one chosen neighbor $z'$. If $x=x_*$ is its trigger, $v$ is not adjacent to any chosen $z'$, because $x_*,z'$ already share $u$. In particular it is plain. Such a test would need $L_v$ in singleton $x_*$, but only a leaf-only destination can supply it. That would stop the singleton before the waiting center $C_z$, so no such test is possible.

For each of the four remaining targets $v$, its trigger $x$ differs from $x_*$. The accepting test must use $h_v$, since dropping its unread center and replacing $v$ by $u$ would give a superset of its other raw positions and accept a nonedge. For a nonplain target, no center-bearing destination could supply $L_v$. Its one chosen neighbor $z'$ assigns the edge to $v$, so $L_v$ is not in $B_{z'}$. Its test therefore has to pass the stall at $C_z$ by supplying $L_z$, then reach a leaf-only destination. All nonplain targets must have the same signature $L_z$, and the single control sequence with that signature can supply at most one of their target leaves in a common leaf-only destination. Those targets are all neighbors of $z$ and a different supporting raw vertex shares at most one of them, while $B_z$ has none.

There are at least three plain targets, and each has two retained noncenter neighbors. If $u$ is the omitted line, a plain target is a line and retains three points, at most one chosen. If $u$ is the omitted point, it has degree three and both chosen lines contain it. Another point on either chosen line has degree four, since two degree-three points share no line. Such a point retains three neighbors with at most one chosen. Points outside the chosen lines also have at least two retained noncenters. In either arrangement the plain targets have accepting destinations by the comparison above, and all of them must have the same trigger.

Along the point or line singleton of that trigger, every plain target leaf must be emitted before or on the common stall at $C_z$. A leaf-only destination would have stopped the singleton at $x_*$ before the stall, so each such emission comes from an offer of one of the $m$ chosen centers on side $B$. Each supplies at most one missing leaf at the fixed trigger. For $m=2$ this cannot supply three. For $m=3$, supplying three means a noncenter trigger loses three edges. It then has just one retained neighbor, already ruled out by raw-support dominance.

## Eleven omitted lines

All points are centers and the only chosen line is $z$.

For point triggers, $S$ lies in the assigned leaves of $z$. The missing lines are noncenters and their extra leaf sets are disjoint for each fixed trigger. Thus at most two misses occur per trigger point, giving at least three distinct targets on this side. Each test must use the trigger's center gate. Replacing its raw point with another member of $S$ would otherwise preserve acceptance, since it only replaces the unread center while keeping $L_z$. A center-bearing destination cannot offer the required target leaf. The line $z$ owns the edges to $S$, and a point-centered destination can supply only line leaves. The tests therefore require one common leaf-only destination, which can contain at most one point target in $S$.

For line triggers, write $J$ for the points with a retained edge assigned by $z$ and therefore a raw leaf $L_z$. If $z\notin S$, all triggers are noncenters. There can be at most one missing point in $J$. Two such targets have the same raw extra leaf $L_z$, each test must use its own center by substitution of the other point, and neither target can be enabled until a leaf-only destination, which can supply at most one missing point on $z$. Thus at least four targets are plain. At most one of them lacks two retained noncenter neighbors, since such a point must have degree three and be adjacent to $z$. There are at least three plain targets with accepting destinations. The singleton runs can emit point leaves only with the one offer of $C_z$ and with a final leaf-only destination. Neither can serve more than one universal target, by the first paragraph of comparisons.

If $z\in S$, then it retains an edge assigned from the common point center $c$ and a different edge assigned by its own star. It loses at most two edges, so at least three misses start from noncenter lines. Their opposite points are plain, since an edge on a line other than $z$ in $S$ cannot meet $z$ again away from $c$. They have at least two quiet noncenters and are not in $B_z$. Their target leaves must all be supplied by the one leaf-only destination, also impossible.

## All lines and two points are centers

Write $z_1,z_2$ for the point centers, $J_i$ for the lines assigned by $z_i$, and call their possible common line $j_*$. A center-bearing line destination may offer point leaves $L_{z_i}$. A center-bearing point destination may offer line leaves only at $z_1,z_2$, in their respective raw sets $B_{z_i}$. If a line is in $J_i$, its leaf is absent from $B_{z_i}$ by unique orientation.

### Line triggers

Let $z_1$ be the common center so $S\subseteq J_1$. A trigger line may have the extra $L_{z_2}$ only at $j_*$. If that extra is present in $S$, $z_2$ is not a missing point, since its only neighbor in $S$ is retained. If $z_2$ is a missing point, it is the only possible centered missing point and there is no $L_{z_2}$ at any trigger line. Missing tests at any line with noncenter points are at most two, since the points add disjoint leaf sets and the singleton has at most two waiting centers.

Keep the noncenter-point tests at trigger lines with just $L_{z_1}$ as their raw good leaf. Each needs to use its own line center, by substituting another such line, since their raw good leaves are identical. If $L_{z_2}$ appears in $S$, discarding that one line leaves at least two other target lines. If the point $z_2$ is missing, discarding that one point also leaves at least two target lines. In either case $B_{z_2}$ contains no leaf from $S$. If neither exception happens, there are at least three target lines, and we can discard the at most one line in $S$ whose leaf appears in $B_{z_2}$. In every case at least two distinct kept target leaves cannot be emitted with a center, as $B_{z_1}$ contains no leaf from $S$. Their successful tests follow one control sequence apart from the disabled target until a common leaf-only destination. Any raw point contains at most one of these line leaves by the common-neighbor bound with $z_1$ and the exclusion at $z_2$. Contradiction.

### Point triggers

Now $S$ is in the assigned leaves of a line $c$. The missing lines have degree three in $H$ and preserve their three edges away from $S$. Any plain missing line other than $j_*$ has at least two retained noncenter points, so its good gate accepts from its destination alone.

For noncenter trigger points, there is at most one missing line in each $J_i$ apart from $j_*$. Indeed, two such tests have the same added point leaf $L_{z_i}$. The test points are distinct, as each shares at most one line with $z_i$. Substitution implies each test needs its own line center. Neither kept target leaf can be emitted with a center, since $z_i$ owns the edge to its line and the other chosen point is not on that line. Both would need a common leaf-only destination, which supplies at most one leaf of a line through $z_i$.

First let neither $z_i$ belong to $S$. The last bound leaves at least two plain missing targets with accepting destinations. If $j_*$ is a nonplain missing line, there are at most three nonplain targets and all the plain targets have accepting destinations. Otherwise there are at most two nonplain targets and only $j_*$ might lack two quiet noncenters. All the plain targets with accepting destinations must have one trigger point $x$, by considering the first emission of one of their leaves on the common singleton sequence. There are at least two such targets, so that first emission $W$ cannot be the final leaf-only destination, which can give at most one at a fixed point. It occurs with the offer of one of the two point centers, say $C_{z_1}$.

Consider any other trigger point $y\ne x$ with a successful missing test. Its singleton cannot reach $W$, since that would enable a plain accepted line at the wrong point. Before $W$ on the sequence from $x$, the only point center that might be offered is $C_{z_2}$. If it is offered, it fires on singleton $x$. Its guard $L_{z_2}$ is then supplied by preceding common emissions, since a raw point provides only line leaves. Thus an added raw point leaf on a true test at $y$ cannot cause an earlier continuation absent from its singleton. Any such test avoiding $W$ must instead fire its own target line earlier, using an emitted line leaf from the offer of $C_{z_2}$. There is no leaf-only emission earlier on the continuing sequence and a line-centered offer emits no line leaf. A target in $B_{z_2}$ is plain unless it is $j_*$, because its edge to $z_2$ is assigned from the line. If it is plain and different from $j_*$, its accepting destination would give a false positive on singleton $x$ with that line. Hence at most one missing edge has a trigger other than $x$. The point $x$ loses at most three edges, which is insufficient.

Next let just $z_1$ belong to $S$. It retains its edge from $c$ and a different edge assigned by its own star, so it loses at most two edges. There are at least three noncenter misses, all on lines that do not contain $z_1$, since the points already share $c$. At most one lies in $J_2$ and at least two are plain with accepting destinations. The latter must occur at the same point $x$. Their leaves can only be offered with $C_{z_2}$ or in the final leaf-only destination, at most one from each. If there are more than two plain noncenter misses this is already impossible. Otherwise there is a nonplain miss $(y,\ell)$ with $\ell\in J_2$, and $y\ne x$, since a noncenter trigger cannot lose three edges and retain only its edge from $c$.

An offer of $C_{z_2}$ on singleton $x$ supplies one of the universal plain targets. The test $(y,\ell)$ must reach this offer along the same prefix. Before it there is no use of the extra $L_{z_2}$, and the target $C_\ell$ is still disabled. Its leaf cannot be supplied by either point center, and no leaf-only emission precedes the offer on the continuing sequence. Therefore raw $y$ alone also reaches the offer, which supplies a universal plain target at a wrong point. This is impossible.

Finally let both point centers belong to $S$. All missing lines are plain. Each chosen point loses at most two edges, so there is at least one noncenter miss. For a noncenter point of $S$, its missing line does not contain either chosen point. Its target leaf can only be emitted in the final leaf-only destination, and it has an accepting good gate. Thus there is at most one noncenter miss. There must be exactly one, at a point $x$, and $z_1,z_2$ each lose two edges. Each retains just its edge assigned from $c$ and one edge of its own star.

The raw support at each $z_i$ consists of $C_{z_i},L_c$ and the operands of $g$. Therefore raw-support dominance forbids singleton $x$ to reach either $C_{z_i}$. Its successful sequence uses only line centers until the leaf-only destination of its missing target. No earlier offer can supply $L_{z_i}$. A line-centered offer supplying it would be at the sole line assigned to $z_i$ outside $c$. That line has no leaf in the raw point $x$ since $x,z_i$ already share $c$. No earlier center-bearing emission can have supplied that line leaf without a point center. Thus such an offer would stall at $x$ and miss the required target.

On a singleton $z_i$ the extra center $C_{z_i}$ cannot run along that prefix because its leaf has not appeared. The singleton must therefore follow the same prefix to the final leaf destination or stop before receiving any missing leaf. The latter would reject its own missing tests. So it reaches the leaf of the missing line at $x$, which has an accepting destination and is a nonedge at $z_i$. This is the final contradiction.

The fourteen-center case is excluded. With five uncovered edges, the remaining thirteen-center case has exactly one spare union. The [spare-union analysis](first-star-five-spare.md) restricts its centers to all lines and one point. The existence of a 58-gate circuit remains open.
