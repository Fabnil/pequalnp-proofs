# Six omitted edges force the star subgraph to span

In [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737), let $H$ be the union of the admissible raw AND stars of a minimum normalized circuit of at most 58 gates. If exactly six edges lie outside $H$, then every vertex has an edge in $H$.

The [isolated-line](first-star-six-isolated.md) and [two-point](first-star-six-two-points.md) exclusions leave one possible isolated vertex, a point $v$. Write $X=N_G(v)$, so $|X|$ is three or four. Every member of $X$ is live and their projected raw supports are pairwise disjoint. The six-deletion [Hall classification](first-star-five.md#the-matching-threshold) gives a matching of twelve edges. The only remaining obstruction to such a matching has all deletions at two lines and cannot isolate a point of degree at least three. There are therefore at least twelve centers. At fourteen or more centers isolation is [already excluded](first-star-six-budget.md#isolated-vertices-at-larger-center-counts).

Use $D(y)$ for a raw support and $c,w,M,\Delta,R,E$ as in the [six-edge budget](first-star-six-budget.md). Write $P$ for pure erased unions, $q$ for mixed attachments and $A=P+q$. The retained count with one isolated vertex gives

$$
P+q+R+E\le 16-c,\qquad \epsilon=A-(w-1),\qquad
\sum_i(r_i-1)=\epsilon-\kappa .
$$

Here $r_i$ counts pure component outlets and $\kappa$ is their total undirected cycle rank. Write $L_y$ for the leaf position of the star centered at $y$ when that center has just one gate. We use the [folding and factoring convention](first-star-six-isolated.md#six-omitted-edges-leave-no-isolated-line), under which an erased wire whose only fork supplies the two operands of one AND can be factored to save a gate.

## A common destination followed by good gates

When $R=E=0$, a destination of more than one position is contained in one raw support. A fully raw-reachable nonadmissible gate $g$ has a rejecting destination $T$ when it is run alone. Otherwise every raw pair enabling $g$ would accept, and its two nonempty raw sets would be disjoint because singletons reject. This would make $g$ admissible.

Suppose at least two tests $(v,x)$, $x\in X$, fire a common first gate and then contain just its destination $T$ together with the good raw positions of $x$. If $T$ were contained in the raw support of a line $t$, then replacing the point by $t$ would reproduce the accepting continuation on two line inputs. Monotonicity gives the same contradiction if $t=x$, since the resulting singleton also rejects.

If the next gate has to be good, $T$ can serve at most one of the neighbors. With a singleton destination the same position cannot match two distinct projected raw supports from $X$. With a larger destination, $T$ is contained in a raw point support $D(t)$, where $t\ne v$ since $D(v)$ has no good positions. Neither $T$ nor the added good raw support can supply a good gate alone. To fire a good gate they must use either $C_t$ from $T$ and its leaf from $D(x)$, or $C_x$ from $D(x)$ and its leaf from $T$. In both cases $t$ is incident to $x$ and can be incident to at most one line of $X$.

## One gate with two raw-reachable operands

Suppose $M=1$ and that gate has operands $a,b$. If $D(v)$ lacks $b$, all $|X|\ge3$ neighbors must obtain it through different mixed attachments. This leaves at most one pure union. No attachment can carry both $a,b$ via its empty operand, since the sole pure union in that case makes a wire with full support $\{a,b\}$ and just one fork. Some missed edge away from $v$ needs raw $a$, so there is an additional attachment. The only possibility is $c=12$, $|X|=3$, $q=4$, $P=R=E=0$. The three line neighbors cannot all obtain $a$ through its single attachment, so $D(v)=\{a\}$. All erased positions are accounted for by $a,b$. After the first gate the three tests differ only by their added good raw sets. A second nonadmissible gate cannot fire first thereafter, since both of its operands would be raw-unreachable. They cannot occur together in the one destination or be supplied by the added raw sets. The common-destination argument rules out these tests.

Consequently $D(v)$ contains both $a,b$. There are at least two pure unions and at least one attachment, the latter for missed edges away from $v$. Suppose $P=2,q=1$. The empty operand of this sole attachment must contain both $a,b$ since they both reach live raw vertices. Denote its support by $S$. Every live raw output using the attachment includes $S$. By [raw-support dominance](first-star-five-saturation.md#setup-and-raw-support-dominance), $D(v)$ has a position outside $S$. It has exactly three positions because there are just two pure unions. If $S$ also had size at least three, both pure outputs would use both union nodes and would coincide. Thus $S=\{a,b\}$. As the empty operand of a mixed attachment in the retained-first fold, it is a full wire support. Only one pure union occurs downstream of this wire in the original OR-only paths, since the other pure union produces a larger support containing the third position. Factoring saves a gate.

All other cases with $P\ge2$ and $q\ge1$ have $R=E=0$ and $q\le2$. On singleton $v$ the first gate fires from its erased support. From then on a continuation can only pass through nonadmissible gates with one raw-reachable operand, using a still available erased position from $D(v)$ and a newly emitted raw-unreachable singleton mate. Each such emission provides no good position. Once a final destination is emitted the run stops. Indeed a support with good positions fits in a single raw support and cannot fire a good gate on its own, and the residual positions are erased. A raw-unreachable position is always emitted as a singleton here, so no gate with two such operands can fire. There is at most one good center and at most one neighbor target leaf in the final support. If it instead leaves a raw-unreachable mate, the mate can use an extra erased position from at most $q\le2$ live neighbor lines. In either form the first-difference tests can serve at most two neighbors, below $|X|$.

## Two gates with two raw-reachable operands

It remains to consider $M=2$. At $c=13$ we have $w=4$ and $A=3$ by the budget. Whenever $w=4,A=3$, all erased components have one outlet and are acyclic. Thus $D(v)$ is private. A missed edge away from $v$ uses a gate avoiding those positions, so $D(v)$ can contain at most the other gate's pair. If it contains the full pair, the private component on those two sources needs two pure unions by factoring, giving positive cycle rank. Each gate therefore needs a fixed missing slot from the line neighbor, and through one outlet it can serve at most one such line. Two gates cannot serve all of $X$. This also excludes $A=3$ when $c=12,w=4$.

We now have $c=12$, $A=4$, $R=E=0$, exactly one orientation per edge of $H$, and $w=4$ or $5$. When $w=5$, $\epsilon=0$ and the isolated support is private. One gate avoids it to serve missed edges away from $v$. The other gate must trigger on $D(v)$, since otherwise two gates with one live outlet per source could serve at most two neighbors. The support containing its pair cannot consist of just those two positions in an acyclic private component. Thus the fifth position belongs to $D(v)$ and is private. In particular every erased position that reaches a live raw output belongs to one of the two fully raw-reachable gates. This last property also holds at $w=4$.

### Cover sizes with twelve centers

If $k$ lines are omitted from the center set, $k$ points are chosen. The omitted lines have at least $4k-6$ retained incidences to these points. If their incidence degrees at the chosen points are $d_t$, we have $\sum_t\binom{d_t}{2}\le\binom k2$. The bound $\binom{d_t}{2}\ge3d_t-6$ excludes $5\le k\le8$, and at $k=4$ it requires equality, with incidence sum ten and all four $d_t\ge3$. This is impossible.

For $k=2$, there are at most three retained incidences over two chosen points, so at most one deletion is outside the omitted lines. The isolated point would have to lie on both lines, leaving at most two incidences to the chosen points because the two lines already share $v$. This would place all six deletions on those two lines and cannot isolate $v$. For $k=3$, there are at most six retained incidences by $d_t\le1+\binom{d_t}{2}$. All deletions would be on those three lines, which must then all contain $v$. They cannot meet pairwise again at any chosen point, leaving only three possible retained incidences.

For $k=9$, equality forces every pair of omitted lines to meet at a chosen point. Each unchosen point has at most one neighbor among those nine lines, so it needs at least two among the three chosen lines. An unchosen point of degree four would use all three and share two with another unchosen point. If they all have degree three, their line neighborhoods are disjoint, which again needs more than three chosen lines. At $k=10$, apart from $v$ there are two live unchosen points with retained edges only at the two chosen lines. If both have degree three they keep at most two edges in all since they have no common line. Otherwise they have at least seven original edges and keep at most three, since they cannot share both lines. In either case four additional deletions are needed, exceeding $6-|X|$. This leaves $k=0,1,11,12$.

### One live outlet per erased position

This includes $w=5$. At $w=4$ the total outlet surplus is $1-\kappa$. Either every component has one outlet or one component has two. If the latter contains the isolated raw outlet, each component still has at most one live outlet. In all these cases, each live erased set lies in a group with a common retained label, so an erased position can be supplied by at most one member of $X$.

Compare with the rejecting singleton $v$. Let $J$ be the number of fully raw-reachable nonadmissible gates that fire there and $K$ the pending tracked count. Each pending track and each remaining gate can serve at most one neighbor by matching an additional raw leaf or erased position. A selected line can also be served by its target leaf $L_x$ emitted on the singleton. At $R=E=0$ each destination has at most one track and at most one such target leaf. A destination can contribute both only through a point center, using one of its raw supports. Thus the combined count of pending tracks and targets is at most $J$ when there are no point centers and at most $J+1$ when there is just one. In the latter case the extra unit needs an emission of that point center together with a target leaf. This is the [target count](first-star-six-two-points.md#tracking-the-targets-when-all-lines-are-centers), allowing one extra emission for the sole point center.

If $k=0$, all $|X|$ tests need to be served by the combined count and the $M-J$ unfired gates, totaling at most $M=2$. If $k=12$, all neighbors are noncenters, so $K+(M-J)\le2$ also suffices to exclude the case.

At $k=11$ there is one selected line $\ell$. At most two noncenter neighbors can be served. Thus $|X|=3$ and $\ell\in X$, and the other two neighbors exhaust the $M$ possibilities from tracks and fully raw-reachable gates. Take the other unchosen point $u$, which is live and can retain only its edge to $\ell$. Its only good position is $L_\ell$. Every erased position in $D(u)$ has a live outlet on the point side and belongs to one of the two fully raw-reachable gates. If this position were absent from the closure at $v$, its gate would neither fire on singleton $v$ nor be supplied by a raw line. This would remove one of the two possibilities needed for the noncenter neighbors. Hence the closure at $v$ contains all erased positions in $D(u)$. The third neighbor $\ell$ must be served by the target leaf $L_\ell$, since the two other possibilities are exhausted. The singleton at $v$ then reaches all of $D(u)$, contrary to dominance.

At $k=1$ write $z$ for the only point center and $m$ for the only omitted line, which retains just its edge to $z$. The combined count gives $|X|\le M+1=3$, so equality requires an emission of $C_z$ together with $L_\ell$ for some line center $\ell\in X$. The retained edge $z\ell$ is assigned to $\ell$ only, and no other line of $X$ contains $z$. Thus none of $X$ has $L_z$ in its raw support. Any live erased position in $D(m)$ reaches $m$ through an attachment with retained label $L_z$, the only good position in $D(m)$. It is absent from all of $X$. If such a position were absent from the singleton closure at $v$, its fully raw-reachable gate would neither have fired nor serve as a first difference, violating equality. Thus all erased positions of $D(m)$ are reached. The singleton cannot reach $L_z$, or it would reach all of $D(m)$. The emitted center $C_z$ therefore remains pending and cannot serve any member of $X$. This also violates equality.

### Two live outlets in one component

The remaining case has $w=4,\kappa=0$, one component with two mixed outlets, and the isolated raw outlet in a different component. In particular $D(v)$ is private. A missed edge away from $v$ uses a gate that avoids these positions. Thus $D(v)$ is a subset of the other gate's operands, and the two-source cycle argument gives $D(v)=\{a\}$. Write $a,b$ for its gate's operands and $d,e$ for the other pair.

Each neighbor needs a fixed missing operand for either gate to start. The two witnesses reach at most three neighbors in total, since there is only one extra outlet. Hence $|X|=3$ and $q\ge3$. At least one neighbor must start the second gate, since the component of $b$ has at most two outlets. If $q=3$, each neighbor has just one attachment. Supplying both $d,e$ to one neighbor through one attachment uses the sole pure union and gives the excluded one-fork pair. Therefore $q=4$ and $P=0$. The neighbor using the second gate uses separate attachments for $d,e$. The other two neighbors $x_1,x_2$ each use one attachment for $b$. Since the erased outlets for $d,e$ already reach the first neighbor, they cannot reach $x_1$ or $x_2$. Moreover the live raw sets of $d,e$ are on the line side, since their respective attachments reach a line.

After firing the first gate the tests with $x_1,x_2$ contain its common destination $T$ and just added good raw positions. The second gate cannot fire from that state unless $T$ contains both $d,e$. Such a destination lies in a raw line support, which the common-destination argument excludes. The other nonadmissible gates have two raw-unreachable operands, so they cannot start either. A good gate is therefore required first, and a common destination cannot serve both distinct line neighbors. This completes the exclusion of an isolated point.

In the six-omission case $H$ spans. The only remaining Hall-deficient possibility has the two lines which retain just their common point. The size-58 question remains open.
