# Twelve centers must all be lines with five uncovered edges

In [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737), suppose a minimum normalized circuit of size at most 58 has exactly five edges outside its admissible raw AND stars $H$, with twelve distinct centers. Then all twelve centers are lines. The unique nonadmissible AND that fires first on the missing edges fires on some singleton point and on no singleton line.

## Tracking additional positions

By the [spanning and single-gate result](first-star-five-spanning.md), $H$ spans and a single nonadmissible gate $g$ has both operands $a,b$ raw-reachable. The number $w$ of erased raw-reachable positions is two or three. The possible third position $d$ belongs to a gate with one raw-unreachable operand. Let $\sigma\ge43$ count oriented star incidences, $R$ repeated actual center positions, $A$ unions with erased operands in the raw ancestries, and $E$ unions outside those ancestries. The [refined projection count](first-star-four-exclusion.md#accounting-for-the-last-union) distinguishes actual center positions and gives

$$
(\sigma+12+R-25)+A+E\le34,\qquad A\ge w,\qquad R+E\le 4-A.
$$

Track the actual good center positions and raw-unreachable operands. A run starting at a noncenter and firing $g$ leaves at most $1+R+E$ tracked positions. We will need this with two units of slack. A later gate consumes at least one tracked position. A previously emitted position that is no longer pending was consumed by an earlier gate, so it cannot be emitted again. Thus only newly reached positions can increase the pending count.

Trace a destination backwards through the unions outside the raw ancestries. Stop at single positions, supports contained in raw ancestries, or external unions already used by a previous emission. The last of these cannot contribute a newly reached position. A dependency graph with $e$ new external union nodes has at most $e+1$ boundary nodes, since it is connected and each union has two operands. Assign each newly reached tracked position to one boundary node containing it. Each nonempty boundary group consists of one raw-unreachable position or center positions belonging to one raw vertex. Across all emissions, the total excess of the group sizes above one is at most $R$. For a center with $m$ actual slots, at most $m$ new positions can be assigned to its groups and at least one group is charged if any of the slots is reached. Each external union is new at most once, giving total excess at most $E$ from the number of boundary groups above one per emission. Together with the one emission by $g$ that consumes no tracked position, this proves the bound. In this argument $R$ may also count repeats only among center slots that actually occur in the run.

## A noncenter with three missing noncenter neighbors

Suppose a noncenter $v$ has degree four in $G$, one retained neighbor $z$ in $H$, and three missing neighbors $X$, all noncenters. The projected raw sets of $X$ are pairwise disjoint because the vertices in $X$ already have the noncenter $v$ as a common neighbor. A mixed attachment serves at most one of them.

If $D(v)$ lacks both $a,b$, then every member of $X$ must supply both. Three mixed attachments are needed. With $A\le4$, there can be at most one pure erased union. With none, the operands must be supplied separately and require six attachments. With one, all three attachments must carry both $a,b$. Folding each reversed fanout with a retained operand first whenever one exists, the unique pure union combining $a,b$ identifies a forward wire with full support $\{a,b\}$. This wire has just one fork along its paths to the two operands of $g$. The [factoring reduction](first-star-cover.md#spanning-in-the-three-edge-case) saves a gate.

If $v$ supplies exactly one operand, say $a$, the three missing neighbors require three mixed attachments for $b$, and $v$ needs a fourth attachment for $a$ that cannot carry $b$. There are no more erased unions, so no pure erased union and no third erased position. The $a$-set $U$ is a group of assigned leaves of $z$, since the sole retained label at $v$ is $L_z$. The $b$-set $V$ is on the other physical side. In each of the three tests at $v$ the common state after $g$ is its destination and the raw leaf $L_z$. The tests add pairwise disjoint good leaf sets from $X$. If the common state rejected, the [first-difference comparison](first-star-spanning.md#support-and-comparison-bounds-at-an-isolated-vertex) would require three waiting centers. But $A=4$ gives $R=E=0$, so the common state starts with at most one tracked position and no further gate can increase the count. Thus the common state accepts. This means every $y\in V$ is a neighbor of $v$. Any missing edge is between $U,V$, yet a vertex of $U$ other than $v$ already shares the neighbor $z$ with $v$. It has no edge to $V$ except possibly the retained edge to $z$. All missing edges would be at $v$, contrary to the five deletions.

Consequently the singleton at $v$ fires $g$. For $w=3,A=3$, each erased position has one mixed attachment by the tight erasure count. The third position can be supplied by at most one member of $X$, just as each retained leaf can. After $g$, each first difference requires its own tracked position, and at most $1+R+E\le2$ remain. For $w=3,A=4$, the pure erased network has at least $w-P$ components, with $P$ pure union nodes. There are $A-P$ mixed attachments and every component reaches at least one, so at most one component can reach two and none can reach three. The third erased position can be supplied by at most two members of $X$. Here $R=E=0$, so the one waiting tracked position can distinguish at most two of the neighbors, even if it is the mate of that erased position. Both cases fail.

We have $w=2$. The three tests now need waiting good centers of three distinct vertices on the same side as $v$, since the added leaf sets have disjoint projected labels. This forces $R+E=2$, hence $A=2$. Both erased positions are attached only on the side of $v$ and through attachments with the label $L_z$, by tight erasure and the fact that $v$ reaches each of them. The initial firings of $g$ on point-line tests are precisely those that select a vertex of that side with both erased positions.

## Excluding the mixed covers

By the [incidence-pair count](first-star-five.md#every-center-is-a-line), if $k$ lines are omitted from the centers, $k\in\{0,1,2,11,12\}$. The case $k=12$ isolates a point in $H$ and has already been excluded. Each of $k=1,2,11$ has a noncenter as in the previous section. For $k=1$ the unchosen line retains just its sole edge to the chosen point. For $k=2$ the two unchosen lines retain one and two edges, all their original incidences to the chosen points, and the line with one retained edge has three missing noncenter neighbors. For $k=11$ the two unchosen points both retain their edges to the only chosen line, since $H$ spans. They cannot both have degree three since they share a line, so one has degree four and has three missing noncenter neighbors.

### Only one center on the side opposite $v$

This includes $k=1$ and $k=11$. Write $z$ for that center, necessarily the retained neighbor of $v$. Every leaf position in the raw support of a vertex on the side of $v$ belongs to one of the good gates at $z$. If $z$ has one gate, its leaf position is already present at singleton $v$. If $z$ is split into multiple good gates, all its center positions must occur in the run at $v$. If even one were missing, the tracking bound with the actually reached slots would reduce $R$ by at least one, leaving at most two waiting positions. Yet all three terminal tracked positions must be centers on the side of $v$. Hence every center slot of $z$ is consumed, and every matching leaf position of $z$ is in the ordinary closure at $v$.

Thus the closure at $v$ contains $a,b$ and every possible raw leaf slot of a vertex on its side. Reaching all the center slots of another vertex on that side would reproduce its entire raw support, which is excluded by [raw-support dominance](first-star-five-saturation.md#setup-and-raw-support-dominance). Every one of the three required waiting centers must therefore come from a different split center. This costs at least three repeats, but $R\le2$.

### Two point centers

In the case $k=2$, write $v$ for the unchosen line with one retained edge and $u$ for the unchosen line with two. Their common point $z$ is retained on both, and the other chosen point $z'$ is retained on $u$. Since all erased positions are on the line side, each of the two missing edges at $u$ requires the singleton at $u$ to fire $g$.

The two missing noncenter points of $u$ add only good leaf sets after the erased positions are consumed. They require waiting centers of two distinct lines. Each of those lines meets $u$ at a missing point, and cannot also contain $z$ or $z'$ by the common-neighbor bound. Its raw support consists only of its center slots and possibly $a,b$. The singleton at $u$ already reaches $a,b$. So raw-support dominance requires both of these waiting lines to be split. This uses up both repeats permitted by $R\le2$, leaving just one good gate at $z$. But then $D(v)=\{a,b,L_z\}$ is a subset of $D(u)$, again violating raw-support dominance.

## Singleton triggers with all line centers

This leaves only $k=0$. If $g$ did not fire on a singleton, no mixed attachment could carry both $a,b$. There are at most four mixed attachments in all. Each reaches a group of points in one star's assigned leaves or a singleton line. A pair of attachments carrying opposite operands on opposite physical sides can cover at most one missing edge. If the line is the center of the point group, its edges to that group are retained. Otherwise it meets the point group in at most one point. With at most four attachments, separating the two operand positions gives at most four pairs of attachments, too few to cover five missing edges.

Suppose a singleton line $\ell_0$ triggers $g$. Its two erased positions can be delivered together through one mixed attachment or separately through two. If any attachment carries both $a,b$, there must be at least two pure unions by the one-fork reduction. This leaves at most two mixed attachments. One of them must carry both positions to $\ell_0$, since a group containing a raw line reaches only that line. At least two missing edges lie away from $\ell_0$. Thus the other attachment must also carry both, to another line or to a point group.

If no attachment carries both positions, two mixed attachments end at $\ell_0$, leaving at most two for all missing edges away from $\ell_0$. There must be one for each operand. Opposite physical sides could give at most one missed edge. Hence the two attachments have their groups on one physical side and fire $g$ on their intersection, which is a singleton line or a point group. In either arrangement $A=4$ and $R=E=0$. The only possible singleton triggers are $\ell_0$ and the second group, and every missing edge has a singleton trigger at one endpoint. A possible third erased position exists only in the case with pure unions, and has raw reach only within the two groups that supply both operands.

If the second group is a line, one of the two lines has three missing neighbors. Its singleton starts with its center and can reach at most two waiting positions after $g$. The three missing point neighbors add pairwise disjoint leaf sets and no erased positions, which requires three waiting centers.

If the second group is a point set $S$, all its points are assigned leaves of one line. The line $\ell_0$ can miss at most two edges. Otherwise its three missing points would add pairwise disjoint good leaf sets, with the third erased position supplied by at most one of them. To supply it twice they would need a common retained label, although their common line $\ell_0$ is missing from their raw leaf sets. This would again require three waiting positions at singleton $\ell_0$, where at most two are possible.

There are therefore at least three missing edges off $\ell_0$, each at a distinct clean line with only its center in its raw support. Each missing point $x$ is in $S$ and its singleton must reach the missing leaf $L_\ell$ of its clean line. On a point singleton in $S$ there is at most one tracked position after $g$. Every subsequent destination offering a center provides no good leaves, and a raw-unreachable position can continue only through its mate, which must be the third erased position. Once a destination containing good leaves is emitted there is no tracked position and the run stops. All successful singleton runs must follow the same sequence of offers to this leaf destination $T$. The support $T$ is in one raw point support, so it can cover at most one missing line at each $x$ by the common-neighbor bound. There are at least three different points in these clean missing tests.

Each clean missing line $\ell$ has three retained neighbors outside $S$. Their tests start with its good AND $h_\ell$, which is later than $g$ because the missing tests require a leaf reached after $g$. After $h_\ell$ these three tests share its destination and add disjoint extra good leaf sets. The operands of $g$ are inert at this point, and the tests do not add a third erased position, since its point-side raw reach, if any, is confined to $S$. A rejecting baseline from that destination would need three waiting centers and can have at most one. Thus the destination of $h_\ell$ accepts alone. A different missing point $x'$ in $S$ also reaches the common $T$, and pairing it with $\ell$ accepts a nonedge. This contradiction excludes singleton line triggers.

The [follow-up](first-star-five-twelve.md) excludes the all-line distribution as well. Any remaining five-edge case has more than twelve centers.
