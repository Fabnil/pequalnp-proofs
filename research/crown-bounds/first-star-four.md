# The four-uncovered-edge budget when every line is covered

Consider a minimum monotone circuit for the deleted-plane graph of [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737). Normalize it and use the [admissible raw stars](first-star-cover.md) to define $H$. Suppose $H$ has exactly 44 of the 48 edges and every line has a neighbor in $H$. If the circuit has size at most 58, then

- its size is exactly 58
- exactly two operand positions outside the admissible stars are raw-reachable
- no edge of $H$ belongs to admissible stars centered at both its endpoints

There are 12 or 13 distinct star centers. In the 13-center case the unique nonadmissible AND with raw paths to both operands fires on a singleton input. In the 12-center case there is one union gate beyond the projection-and-erasure lower count. The four-edge regime considered here is one of the possibilities left by the [three-edge exclusion](first-star-three.md).

## Budget and saturation

The [four-deletion matching argument](first-star-three.md#the-next-budget-case) gives $\tau(H)=12$ when no line is isolated. Let $c$ count the distinct centers, $\sigma$ count distinct oriented center-to-leaf incidences, and $w$ count the raw-reachable positions outside admissible stars. The projection-and-erasure bound gives

$$
s\ge \sigma+c-1+w\ge44+12-1+2=57.
$$

Here $w\ge2$ since the 44-edge subgraph omits edges. Also $w\le3$. There is one nonadmissible AND $g$ that can fire first on an uncovered edge. Its raw-reachable positions are called $a,b$. The same gate must fire first on every uncovered edge. If $w=3$, there is one extra position $d$ reached by raw inputs. It belongs to a different gate $f$ whose other position $e$ is raw-unreachable. If $w=2$, omit $d,e,f$ in what follows. All other nonadmissible ANDs have both positions raw-unreachable.

Call the count saturated if $s=\sigma+c-1+w$. It is saturated in each case with $w=3$, with $\sigma+c\ge57$, or with $s\le57$. At saturation the argument in [the equality case](first-star-three.md#consequences-of-equality) applies. All reversed union gates are used in computing raw supports. Every nontrivial destination support of an AND is therefore a subset of a single raw support. The retained original positions for a given raw output have distinct projected labels, by the connected-subgraph count with original source positions kept distinct. This forces one good AND per center. Write $C_z,L_z$ for its actual center and leaf positions. Each raw vertex reaches at most one center position, even if it also reaches some leaf positions and erased positions.

The saturated erasure count also locates the raw sources that reach an erased position. Let $k_0$ count the isolated vertices of $H$ and let $q$ count the mixed attachments from a purely erased support to a nonempty projected support, restricted to paths to raw outputs. Equality gives exactly $w-k_0-q$ pure empty union gates in the required subgraph. Its $w$ sources and $w-k_0-q$ binary union nodes therefore have at least $k_0+q$ weak components by the edges-versus-vertices inequality, and at most that many because there are at most $k_0+q$ designated sinks. Hence each component reaches exactly one designated sink, either an empty raw output or an attachment. In particular:

- If an erased position reaches an isolated raw port of $H$, it reaches no other raw port.
- For each erased position reaching a nonempty projected raw port, there is a unique mixed attachment by which it enters. All raw vertices reaching it have a retained label in common, coming from the nonempty operand of that attachment.

The second fact implies that a raw input set of size at least two for one such position lies in the leaves of one good star. A center label is reached by just one raw vertex. In particular, if $H$ is spanning, the raw input set to any erased position is on a single physical side, including the singleton-set case.

## Cover choices with 12 centers

Let the 12 star centers contain $p$ points, so they omit $p$ lines. At least $4p-4$ incidences from those lines are on the $p$ chosen points in the original graph. If $d_t$ is the number of omitted lines incident to chosen point $t$, we have $d_t\le4$ and

$$
\binom p2\ \ge\ \sum_t\binom{d_t}{2}\ \ge\ 6p-12 .
$$

The first inequality uses at most one common point per pair of lines and the second uses $6-\binom{d_t}{2}\le3(4-d_t)$. This excludes $3\le p\le10$. If $p=2$, the two chosen points account for at most three incidences with the two omitted lines, below the required four. If $p=11$, the two omitted points each have at least two incidences with the 11 omitted lines. To keep their sum at most four, both points would have to have degree three and be incident to the sole line still in the cover. This is impossible since the degree-three points lay together on the deleted line and have no common remaining line. [Checked graph facts](PlaneRobustCover.lean)

Thus $p=0,1,12$. With $p=1$, the omitted line retains its unique edge to the point center and accounts for three of the four missing edges. In this case $H$ is spanning: isolating any point needs three more deletions of which at most one could already be on that line. With $p=12$, the unchosen point is isolated in $H$.

## Saturation with 12 centers is impossible

Assume saturation and $c=12$. This covers $w=3$, $s\le57$, and the case of an edge covered in both orientations.

### A spanning subgraph of stars

If the raw sets reaching $a$ and $b$ were disjoint, they would have to lie on opposite physical sides, since $g$ fires first on missing point-line tests. For $p=0$ all lines have only their own center label in their projection. The line-side set of $g$ would then contain a single line, so all four missing edges would be on that line, contrary to the hypothesis. For $p=1$ the point-side set would contain all three missing points of the omitted line. These points have pairwise disjoint projected leaf sets: they share the omitted line in $G$ and this is not a center. The attachment property makes this impossible. The case $p=12$ is not spanning.

Thus the two raw input sets of $g$ intersect. They lie on the same physical side. Let $S$ be their intersection. On a point-line test $g$ fires directly from raw supports exactly when the selected vertex of that side lies in $S$. If $|S|\ge2$, all vertices of $S$ share one leaf label from $H$.

For $p=1$ the omitted line $\ell$ has no center, and its three missing point neighbors have disjoint projected leaf sets. The singleton triggers of $g$ cannot be on the point side, since all three missing points would need to lie in $S$. Thus $\ell\in S$. On each missing edge of $\ell$, compared to the singleton test at $\ell$, only the point's leaf set and possibly $d$ are added. Neither raw endpoint contributes a center. The two states after firing $g$ differ by just these extra positions. Subsequent good gates consume their center and may emit at most one center. The only possible additional step not controlled by a center would use $f$: its missing raw-unreachable position $e$ must be emitted as a singleton, and it can then fire only if $d$ is present. Emitting any other raw-unreachable position alone cannot trigger another gate. Thus these runs agree with the rejecting singleton until a first additional required leaf or the position $d$ is supplied by the point. The same first missing position would have to be supplied by all three points. Their leaf sets are disjoint, and $d$ cannot reach all three either by the attachment property. This excludes $p=1$.

For $p=0$, all centers are lines. If the singleton triggers were lines, there would be just one in $S$ and all four missing edges would isolate that line. Thus $S$ consists of points, including the points of all missing edges. At least two points are involved since $H$ is spanning. All of $S$ is therefore in the leaves of a line $c_*$. Each missing line $\ell$ is different from $c_*$ and contains just one of the missing points of $S$. Consequently there are four distinct missing lines, each with three retained neighbors, none in $S$.

On a missing test $(x,\ell)$, the line adds exactly its center $C_\ell$ and possibly $d$ to the singleton input $x$. The position $d$, if present, can be reached by at most one line since distinct lines have disjoint projected labels. Thus for at least three missing tests, their line adds only $C_\ell$. On these tests the good gate at $\ell$ must fire after $g$. Otherwise dropping its unread center would leave an accepting singleton $x$.

Before the first emission of any new leaf positions after $g$, every one of these accepting continuations has the same sequence of control gates apart from the disabled center $C_\ell$. Initially $C_\ell$ is disabled because the raw input of $x$ lacks $L_\ell$. Apart from $C_\ell$ there is at most one offered center, which needs its leaf from the raw point. A nonempty destination containing a center can add no leaves, since it lies in a raw line support except for possible $d$. A destination that emits $e$ offers only the step through $f$ with $d$. All other nonraw singleton emissions stop the run. No continuation can accept before enabling the gate at $\ell$. Thus each success must advance through the same successive offers until the first emission of leaves. This is a common set $T$ of leaves in one raw point support. When emitted, it leaves no other offered center, as the rule that emitted it consumed the previous center or used $f$. The good gate at $\ell$ can now run only if $L_\ell\in T$.

Each point $x$ has at most one missing line with its leaf in $T$. If $T$ lies in the raw leaf support of $x$, there is none. Otherwise the supporting point and $x$ have at most one common line. In particular the at least three tests requiring their own line gate use distinct points.

For any such line $\ell$, its three retained neighbors give tests where $g$ is off and the good gate at $\ell$ fires first. The gate at $\ell$ is later than $g$, because it fires on the missing test only after $g$. On its three retained tests, $g$ therefore cannot run later, either. The destination of the gate at $\ell$ must either emit the output, offer another center, or emit $e$ to use $f$. Other outputs cannot continue with the only center consumed. Another center, distinct from $\ell$, cannot be enabled by the raw leaf sets of three points of $\ell$. If it emits $e$, all three tests must already have $d$. When $d$ comes from lines this can happen for at most one line. When $d$ comes from points, those three points must share a projected label, and it must be the leaf label of $\ell$ by absence of four-cycles. Again at most one line can have this property for a fixed $d$. Thus there is a missing test at one of the at least three lines whose good gate emits the output directly.

Use that line $\ell$ with a different point $x'$ from the group of at least three tests. The pair $(x',\ell)$ is a nonedge, because $\ell$ meets $c_*$ only at its original missing point. The raw trigger of $g$ is the same as on the other missing test from $x'$. Before emitting $T$, replacing that test's line by $\ell$ still adds only a disabled center and no $d$. The same sequence emits $T$, enables $\ell$, and incorrectly accepts.

### An isolated point

If $H$ is not spanning, there is one isolated point $x_0$. Its nonempty raw support consists of erased positions that reach no other raw inputs, by the tight attachment count. The support must contain both $a,b$. If it contains just one, every neighbor of $x_0$ has to reach the other. If it contains neither, every neighbor must reach both. In either situation those at least three neighboring lines would share a retained leaf label corresponding to a point different from $x_0$, violating absence of four-cycles.

If $D(x_0)=\{a,b\}$, just one pure empty union is available in this component of the erased graph. The two OR paths from their only forward fork to the two operands of $g$ admit [the absorption or factoring reduction](first-star-cover.md#spanning-in-the-three-edge-case), a contradiction to minimality. This includes the case $w=2$.

The remaining possibility is $D(x_0)=\{a,b,d\}$. None of these positions reaches other raw supports, so all four missing edges must be incident to $x_0$ and it has degree four. The 12-center cover can have either $p=0$ or $p=12$, since $p=1$ requires three missing edges on one line. With $p=0$, the missing tests after $g$ have $d$ and one center from a line, with no raw leaves. At most one intervening $f$ is possible if $g$ emits $e$. Emitting the output would accept the singleton and emitting only a center cannot help because there are no leaves. Thus the first new leaf set has to contain the leaf of each of the four missing lines. It lies in another point's raw support, impossible since that point shares at most one line with $x_0$.

With $p=12$, the four neighboring lines contribute only leaf sets. Those sets are pairwise disjoint since the lines already meet at $x_0$, which is not a center. The runs after $g$ have at most one center. The guard $d$ is already present on the singleton and every missing test, so a step through $f$ cannot give a first difference. In order to diverge from the rejecting singleton, the four tests would need the same missing leaf in their different disjoint raw sets. This is impossible.

## The saturated 13-center case

Here $w=2$ and $\sigma=44$. We show that $g$ fires on a singleton. Suppose its two raw sets $U,V$ are disjoint. If $H$ isolates a point, the same tight-attachment argument as above requires both $a,b$ at the isolated point, a contradiction. Otherwise $H$ is spanning. The two raw sets are on opposite physical sides, and each lies in the leaves of a clean star if its size is at least two. If one set were a singleton, there would be at most one missing edge between the sets by the common-neighbor bound, or zero if that singleton were the other set's star center. So both are nonsingleton.

The missing edges between $U,V$ form a matching. Each vertex distinct from the other set's star center meets that set at most once, and a center has no missing edge to its own leaf set. Since there are four missed edges and maximum degree four, each set consists of the four leaves of its star. Write $U$ for the points on line $j$ in this configuration and $V$ for the lines through the point center $z$. The two centers are nonincident, since the missing matching uses all four members of each set.

Any 13-center cover of this subgraph with both types of centers contains all lines and one point, necessarily $z$. Here is the count. If the cover omits $k$ lines, it uses $k+1$ points, and those points contain at least $4k-4$ incidences of the omitted lines. The same incidence-pair inequality gives $\binom{k}{2}\ge6k-18$, excluding $5\le k\le8$. The cases $1,2,3,4$ are excluded by the minimum degree three on lines after a matching deletion. For $k=1$ there are too few chosen points. For $k=2,3$ two triples of neighbors would share at least two points. For $k=4$ the four triples would require at least 12 distinct pairs in a set of five points. For $k=9,10,11$ there are respectively three, two, or one unchosen points and the same number of chosen lines. The unchosen points each have at least two neighbors in those lines. The one- and two-line cases are impossible by the degree bound or by a four-cycle. In the three-line case all three unchosen points must have exactly two neighbors. Their original degree was then three, since a matching deletion removes at most one edge at any vertex. Two degree-three points have no common remaining line, which is impossible for two two-element subsets of three lines. This leaves $k=0$ or $12$, and the latter has no line center.

All four edges at $z$ are assigned to its star, and all four edges at $j$ to its star. The initial good marks on each of the 16 tests in $U\times V$ are the line center $C_\ell$, the common leaf $L_z$, and the leaf set of the selected point, including $L_j$. These point leaf sets contain no $L_\ell$ with $\ell\in V$, since the only edges between the two sets are the four missing ones. Following the first $g$, a destination that contains a center may carry $L_z$ in addition, but no new leaf of a line in $V$. The center of $z$ can fire uniformly using $L_z$, and the center of $j$ uniformly using $L_j$. Any other offered center can serve at most one of the four true tests before new line leaves are emitted. For a center in $V$ its leaf is absent, and for any other line distinct from $j$ at most one of the four points on $j$ supplies its leaf. The same fixed continuation through $z$ and $j$ must therefore reach a leaf-only emission that enables the missing lines. It cannot accept before then, since the continuation is the same on all 16 tests. A leaf-only emission lies in one raw support and supplies at most one leaf of a line in $V$. The point $z$ has no such leaves, and every other point lies on at most one line through $z$. No raw line supplies line leaves. It is thus impossible to continue on all four required positive tests.

This excludes disjoint raw sets in the saturated 13-center case. Together with the 12-center exclusions and the budget identity, it proves the stated restrictions. The remaining possibilities at size 58 are one excess union above the projected lower count, or an extra star center with a singleton trigger of $g$.
