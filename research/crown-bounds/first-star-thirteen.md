# Excluding thirteen star centers after four edge deletions

For the deleted projective plane in [L-meta-9737](https://pequalnp.com/desk/row?gap=L-meta-9737), take a minimum normalized monotone circuit of size at most 58. Let $H$ be the union of its [admissible raw AND stars](first-star-cover.md). If $H$ misses exactly four edges and has no isolated line, the circuit has size 58 and exactly twelve distinct star centers. Each edge of $H$ is covered in one orientation, two operand positions outside the good stars are reachable directly from raw inputs, and the reversed OR network has one union beyond the projection-and-erasure lower count.

## The equality case with thirteen centers

Assume there are thirteen centers. The [four-edge budget](first-star-four.md#the-saturated-13-center-case) makes the projection-and-erasure bound tight. The 44 edges of $H$ are singly oriented and each center $v$ has a unique admissible AND $h_v$ with operands $C_v,L_v$. For any raw vertex $v$, write $B_v$ for its good leaf positions. These are $L_u$ for edges of $H$ oriented from center $u$ to leaf $v$. The only possible good center position in the raw support at $v$ is $C_v$.

There is a unique nonadmissible AND $g$ with two raw-reachable operands, $a,b$. It fires first on every missing edge and also fires on a singleton input. The other nonadmissible ANDs have two raw-unreachable operands. Every destination support of size at least two lies in a single raw support, so a destination contains at most one center. The output position and any raw-unreachable operand position can only be emitted alone. Use [consuming evaluation](first-star-three.md#consuming-rule-evaluation), which removes the two operands of a firing gate and adds its destination. Each gate only emits positions of later gates or the output.

The subgraph $H$ spans both sides. If it isolates a point $x_0$, then $x_0$ is the only isolated vertex. The [tight attachment count](first-star-four.md#budget-and-saturation) makes each erased position appearing in its raw support private to $x_0$. If the support has just one of $a,b$, every neighbor of $x_0$ must reach the other. All these neighbors would share a good leaf position, hence another common point, because that other position has a single mixed attachment. This is impossible for even two of the neighbors. If the support has both positions, the single pure erased union gives the fork to the two operands of $g$. The [absorption or factoring reduction](first-star-cover.md#spanning-in-the-three-edge-case) decreases the circuit size.

Now the raw sets reaching $a,b$ each lie on one physical side by the tight attachment count. They intersect because $g$ fires on a singleton, so they lie on the same side. Write $S$ for their intersection. Every missing edge has an endpoint in $S$, and at least two members of $S$ are involved because a nonisolated vertex loses at most three edges. The attachment count puts $S$ in the assigned leaves of one good center $c$ on the opposite side. An opposite-side vertex other than $c$ has at most one neighbor in $S$, by the absence of four-cycles, and the edges from $c$ to $S$ all belong to $H$. Thus the four missing edges have four distinct endpoints on the opposite side. On any point-line test, $g$ is initially enabled exactly when the input from its trigger side belongs to $S$.

## Two consequences of the consuming evaluation

In a continuation after $g$ with at most one good center and no accumulated nonraw positions, the center and its leaf determine the only possible next good gate. A singleton nonraw emission stops such a continuation after its previous center has been consumed. An added set of raw leaves can first change a rejecting continuation only by supplying the missing leaf for a pending center. This also applies when the smaller continuation starts with the destination of a good gate later than $g$ and uses no nonraw positions from earlier gates.

In particular, a noncenter $v$ in $S$ cannot have two missing edges to noncenters $u_1,u_2$. After $g$, each of these edge tests adds only $B_{u_i}$ to the rejecting run on the singleton $v$. The two sets are disjoint since $u_1,u_2$ already have the common neighbor $v$, which is a noncenter. The same missing leaf would be needed to make both tests diverge.

We will also use the following fact about testing a center $v$. Suppose $h_v$ is later than $g$ and has two assigned $H$-leaves $u_1,u_2$ that are noncenters and are quiet in the sense that $g$ is initially disabled on $(v,u_i)$. On each of these tests $h_v$ is the unique initial gate. After firing it, the common state consists of its destination and the raw leaf positions $B_v$, ignoring any early position of $g$ that can no longer be used. The test-specific extra leaf sets $B_{u_i}\setminus\{L_v\}$ are disjoint. A shared leaf would give $u_1,u_2$ a second common neighbor. There is at most one center in the common state, so this state must accept by itself. Otherwise the two accepting tests would require the same missing leaf at their first difference.

Consequently reaching $h_v$ after $g$ on any test containing raw $v$ suffices to accept. Any leaf of $B_v$ already consumed before $h_v$ belongs to an earlier gate and is inert in the common accepting state. Indeed its only possible center came from the destination of $h_v$, and all later emissions go forward. The common state with those inert leaves removed is included in the actual state immediately after $h_v$, which therefore accepts.

## Possible distributions of centers

With $k$ omitted lines there are $k+1$ chosen points. The omitted lines have at least $4k-4$ incidences to chosen points, since all of their $H$-edges must use chosen points. If $d_t$ counts these original incidences at chosen point $t$, then

$$
\binom{k}{2}\ \ge\ \sum_t\binom{d_t}{2}\ \ge\ 6(k+1)-3\bigl(4(k+1)-(4k-4)\bigr)=6k-18.
$$

This excludes $5\le k\le8$. For $k=3,4$, the inequality $d_t\le 1+\binom{d_t}{2}$ bounds the incidence sum by $k+1+\binom{k}{2}<4k-4$.

If $k=9$, the three omitted points have nonempty $H$-neighborhoods among three chosen lines. If one has all three neighbors, each other point has at most one and needs at least two deletions. The deletion budget then forces all three points to have original degree three, and the point with three retained neighbors shares a line with either other point. If all three have retained degree at most two, at least two must have retained degree two and original degree three. Indeed two retained degrees of one would cost five deletions, one such degree uses the entire budget at the minimum original degrees, and with three retained degrees of two at most one original degree can be four. The two retained neighborhoods of size two intersect. Both alternatives contradict the [absence of a common line for degree-three points](PlaneRobustCover.lean#L95).

For $k=10,11$, suppose first that $S$ consists of lines. Each omitted point retains at least two neighbors, since it loses at most one. There are only two or one chosen lines. Two omitted points cannot both have both chosen lines as neighbors, and one omitted point cannot have two chosen neighbors when there is only one chosen line. Now suppose $S$ consists of points. If $k=11$, the sole omitted point keeps its edge to the chosen line and has at least two missing edges to noncenters. If $k=10$, the two omitted points cannot both retain two neighbors. If both retain just one, each uses two deletions and has original degree three. Both points belong to $S$, so their common star center would be an original common neighbor, impossible. Otherwise one retains both chosen lines and the other just one. The latter cannot have the second chosen line as a missing neighbor by the four-cycle condition. It has at least two missing edges to noncenters. The noncenter consequence above excludes both $k=10$ and $k=11$.

For $k=1,2$, if $S$ consists of points, each omitted line retains at least three neighbors on the chosen points. One omitted line cannot do this with two chosen points, and two omitted lines cannot both do it with three. If $S$ consists of lines and $k=1$, the sole omitted line has at most two chosen neighbors, at least one retained. At least two missing neighbors are noncenters. If $k=2$, the two omitted lines have at most four original incidences to the three chosen points by the common-neighbor bound. They retain at least four edges altogether, all at chosen points, so exactly four and all four deletions occur on those lines. A line losing at least two neighbors loses them to noncenters. Again the noncenter consequence applies.

Only $k=0$ and $k=12$ remain.

## All points are centers

If $k=12$, every point is a center and has no raw leaf positions. Thus $S$ consists of lines sharing the leaf position of one point center. A missing edge $(v,\ell)$ with $\ell\in S$ adds only $C_v$ to the singleton run on $\ell$. Its acceptance must use $h_v$. Each such point $v$ has at least two retained line neighbors, all outside $S$, so the two-leaf consequence applies once $h_v$ is reached.

Before emitting new leaves, the target $C_v$ stays disabled. A destination containing a center has no leaves, so every accepting test follows the same succession of single control centers offered after $g$ until a leaf-only destination $T$ appears. Each offered control must fire on all these tests, since the target is disabled and a failed control leaves no alternative. Acceptance before $T$ would omit $h_v$. The set $T$ must supply $L_v$, since after this emission only the target center remains. For any fixed missing line $\ell$, at most one of its missing neighbors can have a leaf in $T$. The set $T$ lies in a single raw line support, and two distinct lines share at most one point. If the supporting line is $\ell$ itself, it supplies none of the missing leaves.

Take two of the successful tests with different lines $\ell,\ell'$ and use the point $v$ of the first test with $\ell'$ of the second. This is a nonedge. The control sequence from the second test still reaches $T$, since its only change is to replace an unused center position. Then $T$ enables $h_v$, which accepts by the two-leaf consequence. This contradicts correctness.

## All lines are centers and one point is a center

If $k=0$, denote the point center by $z$. Let $J$ be the lines assigned to $h_z$. A raw line $\ell$ supplies $C_\ell$ and, exactly when $\ell\in J$, the leaf $L_z$. A raw point $x\ne z$ supplies only line leaves, and $z$ supplies $C_z$ and line leaves $B_z$ from lines outside $J$. These descriptions concern good positions. After $g$, its positions cannot be emitted again.

### Line triggers

If $S$ consists of lines, then $S\subseteq J$, as $L_z$ is the only leaf that two different lines can share. On a missing test $(x,\ell)$ the point $x$ is different from $z$ and supplies no leaf of any line in $S$. The run must use $h_\ell$. Otherwise it would also accept $(x,\ell')$ for any other $\ell'\in S$, whose good raw positions differ only in the unread line center, and which is a nonedge.

An output containing a center introduces no leaf of a line in $S$. A line center can only be emitted with $L_z$, and $C_z$ can only be emitted with leaves from $B_z$. Consequently every successful test follows the same control sequence up to the first leaf-only emission $T$, with its target center disabled throughout. The set $T$ must supply $L_\ell$ when the last control center is consumed. In one raw support there is at most one leaf of a line in $S$. The point $z$ has none, every other point has at most one common line with $z$, and a line has no line-leaf positions. But at least two lines are involved in the missing edges, which is impossible.

### Point triggers

If $S$ consists of points, it lies in the assigned leaves of line $c$. Each of the four missing lines $\ell$ is different from $c$ and has three retained neighbors outside $S$. At least two are noncenters and are assigned to $h_\ell$, since $z$ is the only point center. The two-leaf consequence therefore holds for a missing line whenever $h_\ell$ is reached after $g$. For any other $x'\in S$, the test $(x',\ell)$ is a nonedge.

First suppose $z\notin S$. On each missing test with point $x$, the raw $C_\ell$ is disabled, and the only way the line can affect the run before obtaining its leaf is through $L_z$ when $\ell\in J$. If $g$ emits a line center, its gate must fire on each missing test while $C_\ell$ is disabled. All involved $x$ must then supply that line's leaf, so it is $c$. This step may also bring $L_z$ if $C_c$ was emitted with that leaf. A subsequent offer of another line center at this stage cannot serve two distinct $x$, because no new line leaves have been emitted. Thus the prefix is $g$, optionally $h_c$, and possibly an offer of $C_z$ with a subset of $B_z$. An output before this would already accept the singleton $x$, and an inactive control or a nonraw position could not continue. If a leaf-only emission occurs here, it must enable the target of each success. The same prefix with another involved $x'$ enables $h_\ell$ on a nonedge, a contradiction.

When $C_z$ is offered, if its accompanying leaves enable any missing line, the same prefix enables that line on a nonedge with another involved $x'$, again a contradiction. Otherwise every missing test must fire $h_z$. Its leaf $L_z$ is either present from the common prefix, or supplied by each missing line. It is then also present on every cross-test formed from an involved $x'$ and a missing line. After $h_z$, a center-bearing emission can only offer a line center and cannot add a line leaf. All successful tests follow the same sequence of control centers with their raw targets disabled until a leaf-only emission $T$. At every step the controls use only the point's leaves and the common leaves emitted with $C_z$. An output before $T$ would accept the cross-tests that take this same prefix. If $T$ is emitted, it enables the target on each success. Choose a missing line $\ell$ and another involved $x'$ whose own test follows this prefix. On $(x',\ell)$ the prefix still works with the target disabled and then $T$ enables $h_\ell$. This incorrectly accepts the nonedge.

Now suppose $z\in S$. Then $c$ is assigned the edge to $z$ and $c\notin J$. No missing line is in $J$. A missing line at $z$ cannot be assigned to $h_z$, and a missing line at $x\ne z$ in $S$ cannot contain $z$, since $x,z$ already share $c$. Fix one of the latter points $x$ and one of its missing lines $\ell$. This line adds only $C_\ell$ to the singleton run, so the accepting test must use $h_\ell$. The point $x$ supplies no leaf of any line in $J$. Before the first leaf-only emission, an offered line center from $J$ therefore stalls, even if it brings $L_z$. An offer of $C_z$ also stalls, since no previous continuing offer could have supplied $L_z$, and $B_z$ cannot supply $L_\ell$ because $\ell$ does not contain $z$.

Thus a successful prefix for this $x$ uses only line-center offers without leaves, until a leaf-only emission $T_x$. The same $T_x$ is required by every missing edge from $x$. Because it lies in one raw point support, it can supply the needed leaf of at most one of the missing lines at $x$. The point $z$ has at least two retained edges, one assigned to $c$ and another to its own star. At least two missing edges therefore use distinct points in $S\setminus\{z\}$. Their successful prefixes either emit the same $T$ directly from $g$, or first use $h_c$, the only line whose leaf both points supply. After $h_c$ they must emit $T$ directly since no other line would allow both tests to continue. Replacing the point of one such test by the other leaves this prefix in place and enables its missing line on a nonedge. The two-leaf consequence yields the final contradiction.

This excludes thirteen centers. The [four-edge budget at twelve centers](first-star-four.md#saturation-with-12-centers-is-impossible) then forces one excess union. A [follow-up](first-star-spanning.md) shows that exactly four missed edges already force $H$ to span. The existence of a 58-gate circuit remains open.
